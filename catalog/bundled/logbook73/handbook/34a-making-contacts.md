# Making Contacts

You have met the hobby in [Amateur Radio](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/amateur-radio) and earned your callsign in [Getting Licensed](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/getting-licensed) — and now you are sitting in front of a radio with a microphone in your hand and a heart rate you could log as telemetry. Every operator on the air today has been exactly here. This chapter is the missing phrase-book: what to actually say, when to say it, what a whole conversation sounds like from key-up to sign-off, and what the digital equivalent looks like when the "talking" happens through a keyboard. Keep it open beside the radio for your first week; you will not need it in your second.

## Rule one: listen first

Before your first transmission — before _every_ transmission — listen. This is not just politeness; it is how radio works as a shared space. Nobody owns a frequency, so the only way to know one is free is to listen to it, and the only way to learn what normal sounds like is to hear a lot of normal.

Spend a real evening just listening before you transmit at all. Tune around a band and notice the skeleton of contact after contact: callsigns, a signal report, a name, a location, some chat about equipment, a friendly 73. It is the same ritual you saw in [Amateur Radio](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/amateur-radio)'s sequence diagram, endlessly repeated with local flavour. After twenty overheard QSOs, you will know the dance well enough to join it.

Two listening habits to build immediately:

- **Listen on the exact frequency before transmitting** — for long enough to catch a conversation whose other side you cannot hear. On HF, propagation can hand you only one half of a QSO; a quiet frequency is not necessarily an empty one.
- **Ask before you assume.** On SSB, the standard form is: _"Is this frequency in use? [your callsign]"_ — wait, then once more, then it is yours. Nobody has ever been offended by that question, and plenty of people have been offended by its absence.

One legal note, kept general because the details are your licence's: identify yourself with your callsign at the intervals your regulations require (commonly at the start and end of a contact and periodically during it), use plain language, and keep to the bands and powers your licence grants. Those are among the requirements you must check; prohibited content, third-party traffic and other conditions vary by jurisdiction. The operating phrases in this chapter are custom, not law.

## Answering a CQ: the gentlest first contact

Do not start by calling CQ. Start by _answering_ one — it is far easier, because the other station has already told you they want a contact, they are strong enough for you to hear, and they will do the driving.

Tune around until you hear something like: _"CQ CQ CQ, this is Echo Alfa Four X-ray Yankee Zulu, Echo Alfa Four X-ray Yankee Zulu, calling CQ and standing by."_ When they stop, you transmit — once, clearly, using the phonetic alphabet from [Amateur Radio](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/amateur-radio):

> **"Echo Alfa Four X-ray Yankee Zulu, this is Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie, over."**

Their callsign first (so they know you mean them), yours second (so they know who you are), and that is all. No need to repeat your call five times; once or twice is right when signals are workable. If several stations answer at once and the CQ station comes back to someone else — wait. Your turn usually comes next; they heard the pile-up too.

Here is a complete, realistic first contact, longer than the minimal skeleton, with the parts labelled:

> **EA4XYZ:** "Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie, good afternoon, you are five and seven, five-seven. My name is Ana, Alfa November Alfa, and my QTH is Madrid. So back to you — Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie, this is Echo Alfa Four X-ray Yankee Zulu, over."
>
> **M7ABC:** "Echo Alfa Four X-ray Yankee Zulu, thanks Ana. You are five and nine here, five-nine. My name is Sam, Sierra Alfa Mike, QTH is Bristol, in the west of England. This is actually my first contact, so thank you for your patience! Back to you — EA4XYZ, this is M7ABC, over."
>
> **EA4XYZ:** "Congratulations on the first QSO, Sam — you sound great, and welcome to the hobby. The rig here is a 100-watt transceiver into a dipole at ten metres. It has been sunny in Madrid all week. How do you copy? M7ABC, EA4XYZ, over."
>
> **M7ABC:** "Copy all, Ana. I am running ten watts to a wire antenna in the garden. Cloudy here — no surprise! Thanks very much for the contact, and hope to hear you again. 73! EA4XYZ, this is M7ABC, over."
>
> **EA4XYZ:** "73 Sam, good luck with the new licence. M7ABC, this is EA4XYZ, clear and listening for any further calls."

Notice the mechanics hiding in the pleasantries:

- **The report comes first** and is given twice ("five and seven, five-seven") because it is the one piece of information the contact needs — the RST system from [Amateur Radio](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/amateur-radio).
- **Names and places are spelled phonetically** the first time, then used normally.
- **A clear beginner pattern ends each over the same way**: their callsign, your callsign, "over". That is the baton being passed — "over" means _I have finished, you transmit now_. At the very end, "clear" means _I am done with this contact_ (and "clear and listening" means _but happy to take another call_). Once both operators are comfortable and conditions are good, the wording naturally becomes less formal.
- **Say again**, not "repeat", is the conventional request for a repeat — a habit inherited from services where "repeat" means something else entirely.

## Calling CQ yourself

After a few answered contacts, call your own. Find a clear frequency in the part of the band where your mode belongs (your national band plan shows this; LogBook73's frequency field will also nudge you), ask if it is in use, and then use the classic **3×3 pattern** — three CQs, your callsign three times, repeated in a steady, unhurried voice:

> "CQ CQ CQ, this is Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie, Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie, Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie, calling CQ and standing by."

Then _release the transmit button and actually stand by_ — listen for five or ten seconds. Nothing? Call again. It can take several rounds on a quiet band, and that is normal, not failure. If someone answers but you only caught a fragment of their callsign, come back with what you have: _"the station ending in X-ray Yankee Zulu, please come again"_ — or the all-purpose **"QRZ?"** ("who is calling me?").

Two refinements worth copying from the old hands: slow down (nerves make everyone gabble, and a measured CQ is far easier to copy through noise), and drop the squelch of formality once the contact is rolling — after the first exchange of calls, "over" alone or even just a pause is often enough on a quiet frequency. The full ritual matters most when conditions are bad and at the start and end of each over.

### Calling CQ: a complete example

Here is the missing half of the earlier conversation: this time **you** find the frequency, make the call and lead the QSO. The wording is a clear beginner pattern, not a script you must recite forever.

> **You:** "Is this frequency in use? This is Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie." _(pause, listen, then ask once more; nothing heard)_
>
> **You:** "CQ CQ CQ, this is Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie, Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie, calling CQ and standing by."
>
> **EA4XYZ:** "Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie, this is Echo Alfa Four X-ray Yankee Zulu."
>
> **You:** "Echo Alfa Four X-ray Yankee Zulu, good afternoon and thanks for the call. You are five and seven, five-seven. My name is Sam, Sierra Alfa Mike, and my QTH is Bristol. EA4XYZ, this is M7ABC, over."
>
> **EA4XYZ:** "M7ABC, this is EA4XYZ. Good afternoon Sam. Ana here, Alfa November Alfa, in Madrid. You are five and nine, five-nine. Running one hundred watts to a dipole. Back to you, M7ABC, EA4XYZ, over."
>
> **You:** "EA4XYZ, M7ABC. Five-nine copied, thank you Ana. I am running ten watts to a vertical, and your signal is steady. Thanks very much for answering my call; 73 and I hope we meet again. EA4XYZ, M7ABC, over."
>
> **EA4XYZ:** "Thanks Sam, all copied. 73 and good luck. EA4XYZ clear."
>
> **You:** "73 Ana. This is M7ABC, listening for any further calls."

What happened, in order:

1. **You checked before transmitting**, then called and left a real listening gap.
2. **You read back the answering station's full callsign** before giving an honest report, your name and location.
3. **Both stations confirmed what they heard.** The rig and antenna chat was friendly context, not required exchange data.
4. **You kept control of the frequency.** Because you called CQ, you either invite another caller, call CQ again after a quiet pause, or say you are closing down.

This is a simplex/HF-style conversation, not a repeater script. For the faster portable version, jump to the complete [SOTA, POTA and pile-up conversations](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/activation-scripts).

## What do we even talk about?

The eternal beginner worry — and the answer is that amateur radio comes with a built-in menu. Nearly every casual QSO covers some or all of: **report, name, QTH, rig, antenna, weather.** That is not small talk by accident; each item is genuinely useful (the report and equipment tell both sides what is working, and the weather is a proxy for band conditions). A contact that covers just the first three is complete and polite. One that covers all six is a solid conversation. And when two operators click, the menu is abandoned for an hour of what the hobby fondly calls a **ragchew** — aerials, careers, dogs, the lot.

So what do the conversations actually contain, once they get past the menu? Spend an evening listening and you will hear, over and over:

- **Antennas, always antennas.** What is up, what fell down in the last storm, what is planned next — the hobby's default topic, because everyone has one and nobody is finished. "What are you using at your end?" is the single most reliable conversation-starter on any band.
- **How the band is behaving.** "You are my first contact today, the band seems dead" or "twenty metres has been open to Japan all morning" — operators trade [propagation](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/propagation) news constantly, because each contact is itself a data point both sides are curious about.
- **Equipment and projects** — the new (or lovingly ancient) rig, a kit being built, a repair rescued, something home-brewed. Show-and-tell without the showing.
- **Portable adventures.** Stations activating a summit or a park (the SOTA and POTA award programmes) tell you where they are and what it took to get there; portable operators are usually juggling batteries and weather, so these chats are brisk but vivid.
- **Contests, awards and chasing** — how many countries worked, the grid square that got away, this weekend's contest plans.
- **Local life and completely ordinary things** — the garden, the dog, retirement projects, the roadworks. A great deal of amateur radio conversation is pleasantly unremarkable, and that is the charm: the remarkable part is the ionosphere carrying it.
- **Help, freely given.** Ask a question — about your antenna, your audio, your settings — and you will rarely leave without three suggestions and an offer to listen out for you tomorrow. Teaching newcomers (the tradition calls the teachers "Elmers") is half the culture.

The same menu stretches or compresses to fit the occasion: a contest exchange is four seconds and strictly business, a net check-in is a sentence or two on the night's theme, and a Sunday-morning ragchew can wander for an hour. One practical habit: jot what you learned in the QSO's notes field when you log it — "building a new 40 m loop, ask how it went" — and the next contact with that station starts as a conversation between acquaintances, not strangers. Your logbook quietly becomes your memory of a few hundred friendships.

You never owe anyone a long conversation. _"Thanks for the contact, 73"_ after the basic exchange is completely normal, especially when a station is working through a pile of callers. Equally, if someone wants to chat and you are enjoying it, there is no schedule. The only real conversational rule is the legal one already mentioned: plain language, and identify on schedule.

## Repeaters and simplex: different manners

Everything above describes **simplex** — two stations on one frequency, the classic HF style. Local VHF/UHF operating through a [repeater](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/repeaters) has its own, brisker etiquette, covered properly in that chapter but worth summarising here because it is many people's actual first contact:

- **CQ is normally not used on a repeater.** The equivalent is simply announcing yourself: _"M7ABC listening through"_ — or just _"M7ABC monitoring"_. Anyone wanting a chat will come back to you.
- **To call a specific station:** their call, then yours — _"G4XYZ, this is M7ABC"_ — same order as HF.
- **Leave gaps.** Pause a beat after pressing transmit before speaking (repeater and any links need a moment to key up, or your first word is clipped), and leave a couple of seconds between overs so others can break in — someone with emergency traffic gets priority instantly.
- **Keep overs shorter** than you would on HF; repeaters are shared village greens with timeout timers.

To break into an ongoing conversation anywhere — repeater or simplex — the whole procedure is to say **your own callsign once** in a gap between overs. The next station to transmit will normally acknowledge: "and the station calling in, go ahead." (Avoid the word "break", which in many places is reserved for urgent traffic.)

## A night on the net, start to finish

A **net** is a scheduled on-air meeting — very common on repeaters, run by a **net controller** who collects check-ins and hands the microphone around. It is much more relaxed than most newcomers imagine: the majority are simply a group of local amateurs having an organised chat, and because everyone present chose to show up and welcome callers, a net is the single friendliest place to make a nervous first transmission. Here is a typical weekly club net on a repeater, played from start to finish. The exact wording varies club to club and country to country, but the shape is near-universal.

**The net opens.** At the advertised time, the controller keys up:

> **Net control (G4ABC):** "Good evening everyone, and welcome to the Wednesday evening club net. This is G4ABC, John, acting as net control. The repeater is now open for check-ins — any stations wishing to join the net, please call now."

**Checking in.** You do not tell your life story. You say your callsign — that is the entire check-in:

> **You:** "M7ABC."

If the signal is marginal, phonetics: "Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie." Nothing else is expected yet.

**The controller acknowledges.**

> **Net control:** "M7ABC, good evening, welcome to the net. Please stand by and I'll come to you shortly."

Now you wait. Other stations drop their callsigns in the same way — "G4DEF." … "2E0FGH." … "M0JKL." — and the controller writes each one on a list. That list is the evening's running order.

**The round begins.** The controller works down the list, one station at a time:

> **Net control:** "First over to G4DEF."
>
> **G4DEF:** "Good evening everyone. Name's Dave, I'm in Reading, running fifty watts into a vertical tonight. Had the new antenna up over the weekend and it seems to be behaving. Nice to hear everybody — back to net."

That closing phrase — **"back to net"** — is the net's version of the baton-pass: it hands control back to the controller (not to another station), so everyone knows Dave has finished. You will hear it end nearly every over.

**Your turn.** Eventually the controller reaches your callsign:

> **Net control:** "M7ABC, welcome to the net — first time we've heard you, I think. Please tell us a little about yourself."
>
> **You:** "Good evening everyone, thanks for having me. My name's Sam and I'm in Bristol. I recently got my licence and this is my first time joining a net. I'm on a handheld into the repeater, mostly experimenting with VHF and antennas so far, and hoping to get onto HF soon. I'm listening more than talking for now, so it's nice to say hello to everyone. Back to net."

That is it. Nobody expects a speech; three or four sentences is a full and proper first over, and "I'm new and mostly listening" is one of the most welcome things a net ever hears.

**People respond.** Do not be surprised by what happens next:

> **Net control:** "Thanks Sam, and welcome — always good to have a new voice. Anyone with comments for M7ABC?"
>
> **G4DEF:** "Just to say welcome Sam. If you get stuck with the HF antenna, ask on here — someone will have made every mistake for you already. Back to net."
>
> **M0JKL:** "Welcome from me too. What's the handheld, out of interest? Back to net."

You answer questions **when the controller brings you back in**, not by jumping on top of someone — on a net, all traffic flows through the controller.

**The net closes.** After one or two rounds:

> **Net control:** "Thanks everyone for joining tonight's net — good to hear all of you, and welcome again to Sam. The repeater is now returned to normal amateur use. 73 all, G4ABC clear."

And what did everyone talk about, in between? Exactly the menu from earlier: the weather, new radios, antennas, portable plans, holidays, club events, what broke and what got fixed. A good net is a village pub in which everyone happens to be interested in radio — and next week, when you check in, someone will ask how the antenna project went.

## Three more conversations, played in full

The HF chat with Ana, and the net above, cover the two gentlest registers. Here are the other three you will meet in your first year — each shorter, faster and stranger than the last, and each completely unthreatening once you have read one through.

### A repeater hello

No CQ, no formality — you announce, someone answers, and mobile stations keep it brisk:

> **You:** "M7ABC listening through."
>
> **G4DEF:** "M7ABC, this is G4DEF, good evening — name's Dave, mobile heading into Reading."
>
> **You:** "G4DEF, evening Dave, Sam here in Bristol. You're coming through the repeater clearly. I'm on a handheld at home, just seeing who's about."
>
> **G4DEF:** "Nice one, Sam, all readable here too. I'm about ten minutes from home so just a quick one tonight — but the club meets Thursday evening if you're local, details on the website. G4DEF."
>
> **You:** "Might well do that, thanks Dave. Safe drive home, 73. G4DEF, M7ABC."
>
> **G4DEF:** "73 Sam, G4DEF clear and mobile."

Ninety seconds, no phonetics needed on a clear channel, and note how the formal baton-pass relaxes to just a callsign once the two of you are established.

### The four-second contest exchange

During a contest weekend the bands fill with the QSO at its most compressed. There is no chat — the exchange is a signal report (by convention almost always "five-nine") plus whatever the contest requires, often a serial number:

> **CR6ZZ:** "CQ contest, Charlie Romeo Six Zulu Zulu, contest."
>
> **You:** "Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie."
>
> **CR6ZZ:** "M7ABC, five-nine, zero-four-seven."
>
> **You:** "Five-nine, zero-zero-three, thanks."
>
> **CR6ZZ:** "Thanks — CR6ZZ, QRZ contest?"

That is a complete, valid, loggable QSO — callsigns and exchange confirmed both ways — and contest stations happily work newcomers; your serial number of 003 tells them everything and bothers nobody. Log it immediately (contest stations certainly are), and do not take the brevity personally: on the last hour of a contest, four seconds _is_ the warm version.

### Working a DX pile-up

When a rare station appears — a DXpedition on an island that has not been on the air for a decade — hundreds of stations call at once. The result, a **pile-up**, sounds like chaos and runs on strict discipline. Listen before you ever transmit: the DX station sets the rules, and the most common one is **split** operation — "up two" means they transmit here but listen two kilohertz higher, so callers never bury their voice:

> **VP8DX:** "QRZ, Victor Papa Eight Delta X-ray, up two."
>
> _(you set your transmitter 2 kHz above their frequency, wait for them to finish a contact, then give your full callsign — once)_
>
> **You:** "Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie."
>
> **VP8DX:** "Mike Seven Alfa Bravo, five-nine."
>
> **You:** "Alfa Bravo _Charlie_ — Mike Seven Alfa Bravo Charlie, five-nine, thank you."
>
> **VP8DX:** "M7ABC, thanks. QRZ, Victor Papa Eight Delta X-ray, up two."

Two rules carry the whole culture. First: **if they come back to a partial call that matches yours, finish the job** — correct them clearly, as above, because the contact only counts when both logs carry the right callsign. Second: **if they come back to someone else, stay silent** until that contact is over. Calling on top of another station's exchange is the one way to genuinely annoy a pile-up — and the operators policing it. Patience wins: pile-ups thin out, and the fifteenth call gets through where the first did not.

## The digital conversation: what you actually type

Here is the pleasant surprise about the digital modes: for the most popular one, you barely type at all.

An [FT8](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/ft8) contact is the same ritual — call, answer, report, sign-off — compressed into six structured messages that the software (WSJT-X or similar) composes _for_ you. Your entire physical involvement is a few clicks:

1. Type your callsign and grid square into the settings once, ever.
2. Watch the decode list fill with received messages each 15-second slot.
3. **Double-click a CQ line.** The software queues the correct reply, keys the radio in the right slot, and then walks the whole exchange — reports, acknowledgements, 73 — automatically, message by message.
4. Log the contact when the software offers it.

That is a full, valid QSO in about ninety seconds, and the "conversation" consists of you double-clicking once. The structured exchange, what each message means, and the etiquette arguments live in the [FT8](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/ft8) chapter; the free-text message type (13 characters, traditionally spent on things like `TNX 73 GL`) is the entire scope for improvisation.

The older **keyboard modes** — PSK31, RTTY, and conversational modes like JS8 — are true typed conversations, and they inherit the telegraphy conventions you will also meet if you ever learn Morse (the Morse trainer will take you from zero, including practice QSOs just like the one below):

| You type / see              | It means                                                     |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `CQ CQ CQ de M7ABC M7ABC K` | Calling anyone; `de` = "from", `K` = "any station, go ahead" |
| `EA4XYZ de M7ABC`           | Addressing a specific station                                |
| `KN`                        | Go ahead — _only_ the station I am working                   |
| `BTU`                       | "Back to you"                                                |
| `TNX` / `TU`                | Thanks / thank you                                           |
| `HW?`                       | "How do you copy?"                                           |
| `73 SK`                     | Best regards, end of contact (`SK` = closing the contact)    |

A typed QSO follows exactly the voice skeleton — report, name, QTH, rig, then chat as long as both sides are enjoying it — except that most operators keep a few pre-written **macros** (CQ call, standard exchange, sign-off) and type freely in between. Here is that shorthand in action — the same dance as the voice contact with Ana, in telegraphy dress (this is also, nearly letter for letter, what a Morse QSO looks like):

```
CQ CQ CQ de M7ABC M7ABC M7ABC K
M7ABC de OH2XYZ OH2XYZ KN
OH2XYZ de M7ABC = GE OM UR 579 579 = NAME SAM SAM = QTH BRISTOL = HW? OH2XYZ de M7ABC KN
R R M7ABC de OH2XYZ = TNX SAM UR 559 559 = NAME MIKA = QTH HELSINKI HELSINKI = 73 SK
73 MIKA TNX QSO OH2XYZ de M7ABC SK
```

A few decoder keys beyond the table above: `GE` is "good evening", `OM` ("old man") is a term of friendly respect for any operator, `R` means "received", `=` marks a pause between thoughts, and important items are sent twice because there is no "say again" as cheap as just repeating. (The abbreviations reference in the Toolbox holds the whole on-air shorthand dictionary.) Type plainly otherwise — the abbreviations exist to save keystrokes, not to encrypt the hobby against newcomers.

## The pocket phrase-book

The situations that cover ninety-five percent of early operating:

| Situation                         | Say (or type)                                             |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Checking a frequency is free      | "Is this frequency in use? M7ABC"                         |
| Calling anyone                    | "CQ CQ CQ, this is [call ×3], calling CQ and standing by" |
| Answering a CQ                    | "[their call], this is [your call], over"                 |
| Passing the baton                 | "[their call], [your call], over"                         |
| Someone called but you missed it  | "QRZ? This is M7ABC"                                      |
| You missed a detail               | "Say again your name?"                                    |
| You copied everything             | "Roger" / "copy all"                                      |
| Everything understood, confirming | "QSL"                                                     |
| Ending your part                  | "73, [their call], this is [your call], clear"            |
| Ending but staying around         | "…clear and listening"                                    |

And the short list of things _not_ to say, because they mark the newcomer faster than any fumble: **"over and out"** (a film invention — "over" invites a reply, "out"/"clear" ends the contact; they contradict), **CB codes** ("10-4", "breaker") which belong to a different service's culture, and **"73s"** (73 is already plural — though everyone will still understand the warmth). When in doubt, plain language wins: "understood", "go ahead" and "goodbye" are always correct in any accent, on any band.

## When the mic fright hits

It will, and it is close to universal — there is even a name for it, and operators with fifty years' experience remember their first QSO in detail. Three genuinely effective tricks:

- **Write a script card.** Your callsign in phonetics, the answering formula, the report-name-QTH sentence with blanks. Reading your first overs off a card is not cheating; it is how most people did it.
- **Let the machine talk first.** A first contact on FT8 requires zero voice and two clicks, and it is a real QSO in your log that proves the station works. Voice can be contact number two.
- **Remember the other station wants this.** Someone calling CQ is _asking_ to talk to you, fumbles included. Slow, imperfect and polite beats fast and slick on every band, every day.

## Try it yourself

- **Audit twenty QSOs.** On your radio or a public WebSDR, listen to real contacts on an HF band and tally the skeleton: report, name, QTH, rig, weather. Watch how every operator passes the baton with the two-callsign-and-over formula.
- **Make your script card** and read your callsign, name and town aloud in phonetics until it stops feeling silly. On the air it never is.
- **Check into a net.** Find a local club net (your national society lists them, and club websites always do — [Getting On Air, Country by Country](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/country-guides) has the starting points), listen to one full session, and check in on the next — the walkthrough above is the whole script, and your intro over is three sentences.
- **Log the first one properly.** Whether it is a repeater hello or an FT8 double-click, put it in LogBook73 — the Console page's quick entry takes seconds, and entry number one is the start of the logbook you will still be adding to in thirty years.

## Key takeaways

- Listen first, always: to learn the ritual, and to check a frequency is genuinely free ("Is this frequency in use?").
- Answer CQs before calling them — the other station is loud, willing and will drive the contact. Their call, then yours, then "over".
- A clear beginner pattern ends each over with the baton-pass: their callsign, your callsign, "over"; the contact ends with 73 and "clear".
- The conversation has a built-in menu — report, name, QTH, rig, antenna, weather — and stopping after the first three is perfectly polite.
- Repeaters have brisker manners: announce "listening" instead of CQ, pause before speaking, leave gaps, keep overs short.
- A net is an organised chat: check in with just your callsign, wait for the controller, give a three-sentence intro over, and end it with "back to net".
- Contest and pile-up contacts are compressed, not hostile — give your full call once, follow the DX station's instructions ("up two"), and stay silent while others are being worked.
- FT8 contacts are two clicks and no typing; keyboard modes are real typed chats using the old telegraphy shorthand (`de`, `K`, `73`).
- Mic fright is universal and temporary: script card, FT8 first if you like, and remember the CQ caller wants the contact as much as you do.
