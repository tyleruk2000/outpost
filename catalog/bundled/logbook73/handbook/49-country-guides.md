# Getting On Air, Country by Country

Everything else in this handbook is deliberately global, because the physics and the customs of radio are the same everywhere. But activity is local: the repeater you can actually reach, the calling channel your neighbours actually monitor, the net that actually meets on Wednesday. This chapter is the missing local layer — a starter pattern that works in any country, followed by country-by-country notes for six places where amateur radio runs deep. One caveat before anything else: frequencies, schedules and websites drift over time, so treat everything here as a well-informed starting point and confirm the details with your national society.

## The universal starter pattern

Wherever you live, the first few weeks after the licence arrives follow the same shape. You buy or unbox a radio, program a few channels… and then wonder where everyone is. The answer is nearly always the same: they are there, but concentrated into particular channels and particular hours. Five moves find them:

**1. Program the calling channels first.** Every band plan sets aside a national FM **calling channel** — a meeting point where stations call and then move off to chat, keeping the channel free for the next caller. Program your country's 2 m calling channel and its customary "move-to" simplex neighbours before anything else (specifics per country below). A calling channel that sounds dead at 15:00 is often busy at 19:30.

**2. Program the repeaters you can reach.** Your country's repeater directory (each section below names one) gives the frequency, offset and CTCSS tone — [Repeaters](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/repeaters) explains all three. Start with the handful nearest you; a handheld into a well-sited local repeater outperforms it on every simplex channel in town.

**3. Learn the clock.** Activity is tidal, and the tide table barely varies by country:

| When                                        | What you will hear                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Weekday mornings                            | Quiet — mobiles commuting, retired amateurs, early portable stations           |
| Weekday evenings, roughly 19:00–21:30 local | **The peak**: club nets, casual chats, equipment testing, mobiles heading home |
| Saturday                                    | Portable operators — SOTA summits and POTA parks especially                    |
| Sunday                                      | Society news broadcasts (with check-ins after), club nets, contest weekends    |

**4. Listen for the society news broadcast.** Many national societies transmit a weekly on-air news bulletin read by volunteers, and most readers **invite check-ins afterwards** — an unhurried roll call where a plain callsign and a "thanks for the news" is the entire expected contribution. As first contacts go, it is about the softest landing amateur radio offers.

**5. Find the clubs.** Club websites and their social-media groups announce the nets, the training evenings and the local advice. The best source of local information has always been other amateurs — and [Making Contacts](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/making-contacts) has the complete script for the net check-in that follows.

**The etiquette card.** All of [Making Contacts](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/making-contacts) compresses, for the first month, into two short lists. Do: listen before transmitting, leave a small pause between overs, speak clearly and unhurriedly, give your callsign at the intervals your licence requires, and thank people for the contact. Don't: hold a repeater for long stretches, transmit on top of an ongoing QSO, or worry about making mistakes — everyone within earshot made the same ones, and the operator who fumbles politely is instantly recognisable as one of radio's own.

A suggested first-month routine, adapted from what experienced operators actually tell newcomers: monitor the calling channel and your best repeater on weekday evenings; listen for portable stations on Saturday; catch the news broadcast on Sunday and check in afterwards. And the single most important piece of advice, which no country section changes: **do not wait until you feel like an expert.** Everyone's first transmission feels slightly awkward, the community is at its warmest towards new licensees, and the hardest contact you will ever make is the first one. After that it becomes what it actually is — an enjoyable conversation.

## United Kingdom

The national society is the **Radio Society of Great Britain** (RSGB, founded 1913, **rsgb.org**), which runs training, publishes RadCom, and operates the news service below. The UK repeater directory of record is **ukrepeater.net** — frequency, offset, tone and coverage notes for every licensed repeater.

**Calling channels.** The 2 m FM calling channel is **145.500 MHz**; the custom is to call there, make contact, and move off — traditionally to **145.525 MHz (S21)**, which has grown into a busy general-purpose simplex channel in its own right, monitored directly by many operators. On 70 cm the FM calling channel is **433.500 MHz**. If 145.500 is quiet, check S21 before concluding the band is dead.

**GB2RS: the Sunday news.** Since 1955 the RSGB has transmitted **GB2RS**, its weekly news bulletin, under a callsign issued specifically for the purpose. Volunteer newsreaders around the country read the same script — headlines from the hobby, contest and rally dates, propagation notes — on schedules published at rsgb.org (most readings on Sunday, on 80 m SSB, 2 m and 70 cm FM, and repeaters and digital voice in some areas, with local variations). Two reasons to care beyond the news itself: it is a reliable signal to hunt when you are testing a new antenna, and **most newsreaders take check-ins after the bulletin** — callsign, name, a thank-you, done. For a UK newcomer it is probably the gentlest first voice contact available, because the entire format exists to welcome listeners.

**A London snapshot.** Greater London and the South-East hold one of the densest amateur populations in Europe: multiple 2 m and 70 cm repeaters reachable on a handheld from most of the city, plus active digital-voice coverage. The texture follows the universal tide table — weekday evenings roughly 19:00–21:30 are the reliable window, with club nets most weeknights somewhere within reach, S21 (145.525 MHz) carrying casual chat and radio testing, and Sunday bringing GB2RS readings on several local outlets. If the calling channel seems quiet from a London flat, height is nearly always the fix: a handheld from a balcony, roof terrace or nearby hill transforms what you can hear ([Antennas](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/antennas) explains why height beats power). Because London is where many readers of this book actually live, the rest of this section is a worked example of the universal pattern — the same five moves, applied to one real city.

### London in detail: what to program, who to work, and when

Start with the two channels every London handheld should hold: **145.500 MHz**, the 2 m FM calling channel — call there, make contact, then move off — and **145.525 MHz (S21)**, the busy simplex working channel it moves to (and where a local club net meets on Wednesdays, below). Those two, plus one good repeater, are your first three memories.

**Repeaters worth programming.** The table lists them from the operator's point of view — the frequency you _listen_ on, and the frequency you _transmit_ on (repeater directories usually print the listen/output figure, so both are given to avoid confusion). Access is the sub-audible [CTCSS](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/repeaters) tone or tone-burst the repeater expects.

One to _verify before you trust it_: **GB3EL** (historically East London, 145.600 listen / 145.000 transmit, −0.6, CTCSS 82.5 Hz) is widely reported off-air, and the callsign now also appears on a repeater in Cambridgeshire — check it is actually on before relying on it.

**Nets — where you will actually hear voices.** Scheduled nets are the cure for "London sounds dead": they are when people deliberately show up on frequency. Four active clubs between them cover most of the week:

| Net                                                 | Frequency      | Mode     | When                 |
| --------------------------------------------------- | -------------- | -------- | -------------------- |
| **CPREC** (Crystal Palace R&EC)                     | 145.525 (S21)* | FM       | Wed 20:00            |
| **CPREC** 6 m                                       | 51.55          | FM       | Sat 20:00            |
| **CVRS** (Cray Valley RS) 2 m                       | 145.350        | FM       | Sun 10:00            |
| **CVRS** 4 m                                        | 70.425         | FM       | Fri 20:00            |
| **CVRS** Top Band                                   | ~1.905         | SSB → AM | 1st Mon of the month |
| **SRCC** (Surrey Radio Contact Club, "Croydon ARC") | 144.6125       | D‑Star   | Fri 19:30            |
| **SRCC** 2 m                                        | 145.350        | FM       | Fri 20:00            |

\*CPREC's Wednesday net is on S21 "or the nearest free simplex channel", so listen around 145.525 if it is busy. SRCC also runs 6 m (Tue 20:00, 51.51 FM), 4 m (Thu 20:00, 70.30 FM) and a 160 m net (Sun 09:30, 1.905 LSB). Pick whichever club is nearest — **CPREC** around Crystal Palace and SE‑central London (cprec.org), **CVRS** at Eltham in the south-east (cvrs.uk), **SRCC** at Croydon in the south (srcc.uk), and **Sutton & Cheam RS** in the south-west, who keep GB3XP (scrs.org.uk). A net check-in — callsign, name, a thank-you — is the gentlest first contact there is, and the fastest way to find a local Elmer.

**APRS.** In the UK and the rest of Europe, [APRS](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/aprs) lives on **144.800 MHz** FM — _not_ the 144.390 MHz you will see in US guides, so do not copy that across. Before transmitting, open **aprs.fi** and search your area: no licence is needed just to watch, and you will see the live local igates, digipeaters and heard stations on a map — proof of how much is moving locally, and which gateway is hearing your patch. Central London has dense igate coverage, so even a modest beacon usually gets gated to the internet. (A newer LoRa‑APRS network is also appearing around 439.9125 MHz, but the 144.800 FM network is still the mainstream one.)

**A note of London history.** The Crystal Palace mast is one of the highest sites in South London, and the legendary 2 m repeater there — **GB3LO** (on air from 1975), later renamed **GB3SL** — reputedly covered the great majority of the area inside the M25. That is the machine behind the "you can hit it from anywhere" reputation; today's GB7LO DMR at Beckenham is a _different_ repeater, so keep the two straight if you read the older club histories.

Everything here drifts — repeaters change tone, move site or go off-air, and net nights shift. The authoritative live source for UK repeaters is **ukrepeater.net** (the RSGB's ETCC directory); punch in your postcode for everything reachable, by distance, with current frequencies, offsets and tones, and confirm before you rely on any figure above.

## United States

The national society is the **ARRL** (American Radio Relay League, founded 1914, **arrl.org**). The commonly used repeater directories are **RepeaterBook** and the ARRL's own directory.

**Calling channels.** The national 2 m FM simplex calling frequency is **146.520 MHz** — universally programmed, especially by mobile and travelling operators — with **446.000 MHz** its 70 cm counterpart. American amateur culture is strongly repeater-centred: enormous linked systems, daily commuter nets, and severe-weather nets (SKYWARN) that activate when storms do.

**On-air bulletins.** The ARRL's headquarters station **W1AW** transmits scheduled bulletins and Morse practice sessions on published HF frequencies — long the traditional way to check a new HF receiver and, for many, the way they learned code. **Field Day**, on the fourth full weekend of June, is the biggest operating event on the continent: thousands of clubs on the air from parks and fields, explicitly welcoming visitors and new licensees to take the microphone.

**A Washington, D.C. snapshot.** The capital region (D.C., Maryland, Northern Virginia) is dense with clubs and repeaters, many with daily nets; government and engineering careers have seeded the area with amateurs for a century. As everywhere in the US, the local club's weekly net is the front door.

## Canada

The national society is **Radio Amateurs of Canada** (RAC, **rac.ca**). Canadian band plans and customs align closely with the US — **146.520 MHz** serves as the 2 m FM simplex calling frequency, RepeaterBook covers Canadian repeaters well, and repeater nets are the social backbone, with the added Canadian flavour that VHF coverage matters enormously across long, thinly populated distances; well-sited repeaters and linked systems carry regions the size of European countries.

**An Ottawa snapshot.** The capital supports several active clubs and repeaters with regular nets, bilingual on-air culture in the region (English and French), and easy access to both Ontario and Québec amateur communities across the river.

## France

The national society is the **REF** (Réseau des Émetteurs Français, **r-e-f.org**). France follows the IARU Region 1 band plan, so the 2 m FM calling channel is the familiar **145.500 MHz**, with the same call-then-move custom as the UK. French operating culture keeps strong HF and contesting traditions, active digital-voice networks, and a healthy SOTA scene in the Alps and Pyrénées.

**A Paris snapshot.** Île-de-France has multiple repeaters across 2 m, 70 cm and digital voice reachable from most of the city, and several clubs with regular meetings. French-language nets predominate, as you would expect — but a polite English-language call rarely goes unanswered, and trying your French, however rusty, is warmly received.

## Spain

The national society is the **URE** (Unión de Radioaficionados Españoles, **ure.es**), with a strong network of local sections that double as clubs. Spain is also IARU Region 1: **145.500 MHz** is the 2 m FM calling channel with the usual move-off custom. Spanish amateurs are famously active on HF — EA callsigns are everywhere in contests and pile-ups — and the URE sections run regular nets and training.

**A Madrid snapshot.** The capital has active repeaters on 2 m, 70 cm and digital voice, and URE Madrid section activity to plug into. If you worked through [Making Contacts](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/making-contacts), you have already met Madrid: Ana's end of that first QSO is a fair picture of the welcome.

## Japan

The national society is the **JARL** (Japan Amateur Radio League, **jarl.org**), and Japan is one of the great amateur radio nations — for decades it licensed more amateurs than any other country, and it manufactures much of the world's amateur equipment. Japanese band plans differ from both Europe and the Americas (the 2 m band is 144–146 MHz, with **145.000 MHz** customarily used as the FM calling frequency), so a newcomer's first stop should be the JARL band plan rather than assumptions imported from elsewhere.

**A Tokyo snapshot.** Tokyo's scene is unlike anywhere else: the Akihabara electronics district remains a pilgrimage for radio equipment, the JARL **Ham Fair** — held annually in the Tokyo area — is one of the largest amateur radio gatherings in the world, and the metropolitan region supports extensive repeater and digital-voice coverage. Operating is predominantly in Japanese, but JA operators are enthusiastic DXers and contest participants, and English exchanges of the report-name-QTH kind are routine on HF.

## Everywhere else

No section for your country? The pattern still holds, and two steps localise it in an evening. First, find your national society through the **International Amateur Radio Union** (**iaru.org**), which lists its member society for nearly every country — that society's website carries the band plan (the Toolbox's band plan reference has the IARU segments by region), the news broadcast schedule if one exists, and the club list. Second, apply the universal starter pattern: calling channel, nearest repeaters, weekday evenings, Saturday portables, Sunday bulletins. The tide table is remarkably international.

## Try it yourself

- **Program the trio tonight**: your national 2 m calling channel, its move-to neighbour, and the strongest local repeater from your national directory. Save them as memories 1–3.
- **Catch one news broadcast.** Find your society's schedule (GB2RS at rsgb.org for the UK; W1AW at arrl.org for the US), listen through once, and if check-ins are taken — check in. Log it in LogBook73: a real first contact.
- **Build the weekly routine** for one month: calling channel + repeater on weekday evenings, portables on Saturday, bulletin on Sunday. Keep notes on the scratch pad about who and what you heard; patterns appear within a fortnight.

## Key takeaways

- Activity is concentrated, not absent: calling channels, local repeaters, weekday evenings around 19:00–21:30, portables on Saturday, bulletins and nets on Sunday.
- Program the national calling channel and its move-to neighbour first — in the UK that is 145.500 MHz and 145.525 (S21); in North America, 146.520 MHz.
- Society news broadcasts that take check-ins afterwards — GB2RS in the UK is the model — are the gentlest first voice contact in the hobby.
- Every country section reduces to the same three resources: the national society, the repeater directory, and the nearest club.
- Details drift — confirm frequencies and schedules with your national society, found via iaru.org if you don't know it.
- The hardest contact you will ever make is the first one; the community is at its warmest towards exactly the people making it.
