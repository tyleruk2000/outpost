# CW: keyer, decoder, messages and QSK

If the (tr)uSDX has a native language, it is Morse. A constant-envelope mode on a constant-envelope transmitter: the class-E PA runs flat out at its efficient best, five watts becomes a genuinely competitive signal, and the whole menu group 2 — keyer, decoder, QSK, practice mode — plus the group 4 message memories turn this pocket radio into a complete CW station. This chapter takes them in the order a CW operator meets them.

## Plugging in a key

The key/mic jack accepts either kind of key:

- **A paddle** — two contacts, dit and dah, driven by the built-in electronic keyer. This is the intended experience.
- **A straight key** — set `2.6 Keyer Mode` to **Straight** and the radio simply follows your hand. (Sideswipers and external keyers also want Straight mode: the radio keys when the line closes, no cleverness.)

And one glorious fallback: **in CW mode the PTT line acts as an emergency straight key** — a documented feature, not an accident. A speaker-mic button, or two wires touched together, will send Morse the day your paddle stays home.

## The keyer: `2.5`, `2.6`, `2.7`

**`2.5 Keyer Speed`** sets the paddle keyer's speed in words per minute (the manual documents roughly `10–40` WPM on current builds). Set it to the speed you can _copy_, not the speed you admire. Changing it mid-session is a quick-menu favourite.

**`2.6 Keyer Mode`** picks the keyer's logic:

- **Iambic-A** and **Iambic-B** both alternate dit-dah while you squeeze both paddles; they differ in what happens at the instant you let go. Mode B inserts one more opposite element after release; mode A stops cleaner. If you learned on one, the other feels haunted — set the one your fingers already know. If you are new: pick either, stay with it, and let your hands calibrate.
- **Straight** — no keyer; for straight keys and external keyers as above.

**`2.7 Keyer Swap`** exchanges dit and dah electrically. If your paddle sends dah when your thumb wanted dit, this fixes it in two seconds — far better than rewiring a plug at a summit.

## Semi QSK: `2.4`

QSK — break-in — is how quickly your receiver comes back between the elements you send. Full QSK (hearing the band _inside_ your own words) is the luxury of relay-free big rigs; the (tr)uSDX offers **semi QSK**: with `2.4` ON, the radio returns to receive in the short gaps of your sending rhythm, so a station trying to interrupt you gets heard at the next pause. OFF holds transmit longer between elements — a calmer experience on hardware and ears, at the cost of responsiveness.

Recommendation: **ON**, and learn to _listen in your gaps_ — it is the difference between transmitting Morse and having a conversation in it.

## The sidetone and the filter: `2.2` and `1.3`

Builds that expose **`2.2 CW Tone`** offer a choice of sidetone pitch (`600` or `700 Hz`). This is also the pitch the receive filtering centres on, and — crucially — **the pitch at which you should tune stations in**. The habit that makes narrow filters usable: tune a CW signal until its note matches your sidetone pitch. Now it sits in the middle of your filter, and you can tighten `1.3 Filter BW` from `500 Hz` down through `200` to `100` without losing them. If a narrow filter always seems to "lose" the station, you are tuning by ear to the wrong pitch — match the sidetone.

## The decoder: `2.1`

With `2.1 CW Decoder` ON, the radio prints its best reading of received Morse across the display. Understand its character and it becomes a real tool:

- It thrives on **clean, machine-like sending at a steady speed** — it will happily transcribe automated beacons and contest exchanges.
- It struggles with **fists**: irregular hand-sent spacing, drifting speed, QSB fades and pile-up chaos produce cheerful nonsense.
- It is a **learning accelerator**, not a crutch: copy in your head first, then glance at the screen to check yourself. Your ear will outgrow it — that is the plan. [CW Operating](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/cw-operating) has the full learning path.

The decoder needs the signal tuned to the filter centre (the sidetone-matching habit again) and copes best with the `500 Hz` filter or narrower.

## Practice mode: `2.8`

`2.8 Practice` ON gives you the whole radio — keyer, sidetone, decoder — with **no RF output**. It is the best Morse practice machine you own: send with the paddle and watch the decoder grade your fist in real time. If the decoder prints what you meant, a human will copy you too.

The one warning is the obvious one: **practice mode stays on until you turn it off.** If you ever call CQ into a strangely silent world with perfect SWR and zero forward power, check `2.8` before suspecting the antenna. (Recent beta firmware experimented with removing this item — on those builds, setting `3.3 TX Drive` to `0` achieves the same silence. Same idea, same trap, same checklist entry.)

## Messages: menu group 4

The radio can key stored messages itself:

- **`4.2 CQ Message`** holds your CQ — something like `CQ CQ DE M7ABC M7ABC K` — entered character by character with the encoder (tedious once, useful forever).
- **`4.1 CQ Interval`** (`0–60 s`) sets the pause after the message before it automatically repeats — turning your CQ into a patient beacon-style caller. Touching the key stops the cycle and hands you the contact.
- **`4.3`–`4.7` CW Message 2–6** exist on builds with extended message memories (present in the current beta series): stash your exchange, `TU 73`, a contest string. On message-capable firmware, a left-button press in message mode plays the selected slot.

Etiquette notes: an auto-repeating CQ is a robot with your callsign on it — stay at the radio, answer promptly, and never leave it calling unattended (which is both rude and, in most jurisdictions, not a licensed mode of operation). Pick a repeat interval long enough to actually listen in: `4`–`10 s`.

## A complete CW contact, (tr)uSDX edition

1. **Setup once:** paddle in, `2.6` mode set, `2.7` swap correct, `2.5` at your speed, `2.4 Semi QSK` ON, `2.1 Decoder` ON, `2.2` sidetone to taste, filter at `500 Hz`. `2.8 Practice` OFF — deliberately.
2. **Find or make a frequency:** tune the CW segment (low end of the band); match received notes to your sidetone. Listen, send `QRL?` (is this in use?), listen again.
3. **Call:** paddle or message memory — `CQ CQ DE <call> <call> K`.
4. **Work them:** report, name, QTH — or just `599 TU` in a contest. Semi QSK lets you catch a repeat request mid-over.
5. **Log it.** Five watts, a wire, and a mode invented before valves were reliable — logged like any kilowatt contact, and sweeter.

## Why CW is this radio's best mode, in numbers

A `500 Hz` filter passes a fiftieth of the noise of a voice channel; concentrated in that slot, your five watts buy the readability that fifty watts of SSB struggles to. The [CW chapter of the handbook](outpost:entry/logbook73.handbook/cw-operating) makes the general case — the (tr)uSDX simply makes it _portable_. If you only master one transmitting mode on this radio, master this one; [chapter 11](outpost:entry/logbook73.guide.trusdx/configs) has a ready-made SOTA CW configuration to start from.
