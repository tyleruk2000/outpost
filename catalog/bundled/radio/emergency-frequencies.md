# Emergency, distress and calling frequencies

The frequencies set aside internationally for distress and safety at sea and in the air, the 406 MHz beacon band, and the calling and emergency frequencies used by radio amateurs, with a note of who may transmit on each. Frequencies are from the UK and United States regulators and the International Amateur Radio Union (IARU).

## The law in brief

Almost everything on this sheet belongs to a licensed service. Transmitting on a marine or aeronautical frequency needs the licence for that service, amateur frequencies need an amateur licence, and a false distress signal is a serious offence everywhere. Rules differ by country. This sheet is a reference, not legal advice, and it does not tell you that an unlicensed transmission would be lawful in an emergency: it quotes what the regulators themselves say, below.

A working telephone is almost always the faster way to reach the emergency services. Knowing these frequencies is mainly useful for listening, for licensed operators, and for understanding what rescue services use.

## What the regulators say about distress and emergencies

Ofcom (UK), on distress frequencies: "Distress calls take absolute priority over all other transmissions; anyone receiving a distress call must immediately cease any transmissions that may interfere with the call and listen on the frequency used for the call."

Ofcom's amateur licence, Condition 7(5): the licensee "may use or permit the use of the Radio Equipment to assist with communications: a) in times of disaster or local, national, international emergency; b) to support operations conducted by a user service; or c) during any exercise relating to a and b."

FCC amateur rules (United States), section 97.403: "No provision of these rules prevents the use by an amateur station of any means of radiocommunication at its disposal to provide essential communication needs in connection with the immediate safety of human life and immediate protection of property when normal communication systems are not available." Section 97.405 says the same for an amateur station in distress and for a station helping it.

FCC CB rules, section 95.931: "Any CBRS channel may be used for emergency communications or for traveler assistance", and operators "must, at all times and on all channels, give priority to emergency communications".

FCC GMRS rules, section 95.1705: a licence holder "may allow anyone to operate his or her GMRS station if necessary to communicate an emergency message".

| 16420 kHz | Distress and safety by radiotelephony |
| 4207.5, 6312, 8414.5, 12577 and 16804.5 kHz | DSC distress and safety calls |

| 490 kHz | National NAVTEX |

The status of 2182 kHz. It is still listed as an international distress frequency by both Ofcom and the FCC, but far fewer stations listen on it than once did. The United States Coast Guard stopped keeping a watch on 2182 kHz, stopped guarding DSC on 2187.5 kHz and stopped its 2670 kHz broadcasts on 1 August 2013, and pointed mariners to the HF distress frequencies it monitors (4125, 6215, 8291 and 12290 kHz) and to DSC and satellite systems. In the UK, HM Coastguard still announces its scheduled MF weather and safety broadcasts on 2182 kHz. Do not rely on a voice call on 2182 kHz being heard.

## 406 MHz distress beacons

| Item | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Frequency band | 406.0 to 406.1 MHz, from the beacon up to satellites |
| Types | EPIRBs for vessels, ELTs for aircraft, and personal locator beacons (PLBs) |
| Registration | In the UK "you must register your beacon and keep your registration up to date" |
| Use | Distress only. The beacon sends an alert through satellites to search and rescue authorities |

## Amateur radio emergency centres of activity

Voluntary, and for licensed amateurs. IARU Region 1 says they "are not 'absolute' frequencies but instead are 'centres of activity'", with emergency traffic possibly within 20 kHz either side. Region 1 is Europe, Africa, the Middle East and northern Asia; Region 2 is the Americas; Region 3 is the rest of Asia and the Pacific.

| Band | Region 1 | Region 2 | Region 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 80 m | 3760 kHz | 3750 or 3985 kHz | 3600 kHz |
| 40 m | 7110 kHz | 7060, 7240 or 7275 kHz | 7110 kHz |
| 20 m | 14300 kHz | 14300 kHz | 14300 kHz |
| 17 m | 18160 kHz | 18160 kHz | 18160 kHz |
| 15 m | 21360 kHz | 21360 kHz | 21360 kHz |

IARU Region 1 also lists frequencies that individual countries use. For Great Britain it gives 3.663 MHz, 3.760 MHz and 5.2785 MHz among others; see the source for the full list.

## Amateur radio calling frequencies

Calling frequencies are where amateurs listen for a first contact before moving elsewhere. They come from voluntary band plans, there is no guarantee anyone is listening, and they need an amateur licence.

| Frequency | Where | Use in the band plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 29.600 MHz | Regions 1 and 2 | FM calling |
| 51.510 MHz | Region 1 | FM calling |
| 70.450 MHz | Region 1 (the UK has a 70 MHz band) | FM calling |
| 144.300 MHz | Region 1 | SSB centre of activity |
| 145.500 MHz | Region 1, including the UK | FM calling |
| 144.200 MHz | Region 2 | CW and SSB weak-signal calling |
| 146.520 MHz | Region 2, including the United States | FM calling |
| 432.100 MHz | Region 2 | CW and SSB weak-signal calling |
| 1294.500 MHz | Region 2 | FM simplex calling |

Alaska has one frequency set by rule, not by band plan: 5167.5 kHz (the FCC amateur rules give it as the channel at 5.1675 MHz), single sideband and no more than 150 W PEP, for emergency communications by stations in or within 92.6 km of Alaska.

## Radio you can use without an amateur licence

| Service | Frequency | What the rules say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CB, United States | Channel 9, 27.065 MHz | "May be used only for emergency communications or traveler assistance" |
| CB, UK | No channel is reserved | The UK rules read for this sheet set no emergency channel |
| PMR446, FRS, MURS | No channel is reserved | The rules read for this sheet set no emergency or calling channel |

Nobody is required to monitor any of these, in either country.

## Who may transmit

| Frequencies | Who |
| --- | --- |
| Marine VHF, MF and HF distress and calling | Licensed ship and coast stations, and aircraft in search and rescue |
| 121.5 MHz, 123.1 MHz, 243 MHz | Aircraft and aeronautical stations, survival craft, and distress beacons |
| 406.0 to 406.1 MHz | Registered distress beacons only |
| Amateur centres of activity and calling frequencies | Licensed radio amateurs, within their own country's bands |
| CB, PMR446, FRS, MURS | Anyone, with equipment that meets the rules of that service in that country |
| GMRS | FCC licence holders and their families; anyone, to pass an emergency message with the licence holder's permission |
