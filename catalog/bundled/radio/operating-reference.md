# Radio operating reference

The things an operator looks up most: the phonetic alphabet, Morse code, the Q code, signal reports, band names, and turning frequency into wavelength. Each table is taken from the international body or agency that publishes it.

## The law in brief

Nothing here gives permission to transmit: that depends on the licence and the service you are using, and differs by country. This sheet is a reference, not legal advice.

Timing: a dash is equal to three dots. The space between the parts of one letter is one dot; between two letters, three dots; between two words, seven dots.

## Q code

The Q code is a set of three-letter abbreviations, each with a meaning as a question and as a statement. The meanings below are those in the ITU's recommendation for the maritime mobile service. Sent with a question mark a code is a question; alone, it is the statement.

| Code | As a question | As a statement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| QRA | What is the name of your vessel (or station)? | The name of my vessel (or station) is ... |
| QRG | Will you tell me my exact frequency? | Your exact frequency is ... kHz (or MHz) |
| QRK | What is the intelligibility of my signals? | The intelligibility of your signals is ... |
| QRL | Are you busy? | I am busy. Please do not interfere |
| QRM | Is my transmission being interfered with? | Your transmission is being interfered with |
| QRN | Are you troubled by static? | I am troubled by static |
| QRO | Shall I increase transmitter power? | Increase transmitter power |
| QRP | Shall I decrease transmitter power? | Decrease transmitter power |
| QRQ | Shall I send faster? | Send faster (... words per minute) |
| QRS | Shall I send more slowly? | Send more slowly (... words per minute) |
| QRT | Shall I stop sending? | Stop sending |
| QRU | Have you anything for me? | I have nothing for you |
| QRV | Are you ready? | I am ready |
| QRX | When will you call me again? | I will call you again at ... hours on ... kHz (or MHz) |
| QRZ | Who is calling me? | You are being called by ... |
| QSA | What is the strength of my signals? | The strength of your signals is ... |
| QSB | Are my signals fading? | Your signals are fading |
| QSK | Can you hear me between your signals, and if so may I break in on your transmission? | I can hear you between my signals; break in on my transmission |
| QSL | Can you acknowledge receipt? | I am acknowledging receipt |
| QSO | Can you communicate with ... direct (or by relay)? | I can communicate with ... direct (or by relay through ...) |
| QSP | Will you relay to ... free of charge? | I will relay to ... free of charge |
| QSY | Shall I change to transmission on another frequency? | Change to transmission on another frequency |
| QTH | What is your position in latitude and longitude (or according to any other indication)? | My position is ... |
| QTR | What is the correct time? | The correct time is ... hours |

Radio amateurs also use some codes as everyday words. The IARU's operating guide gives QRG as "frequency", QSL as "I confirm", QSO as "radio contact" and QTH as "location", and 73 as "best regards".

## Signal reports: RST

A report is two figures for voice (readability and strength, for example "five nine") and three for Morse (readability, strength and tone, for example 599). The scales are as given in the IARU's operating guide.

| R | Readability |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Unreadable |
| 2 | Barely readable, occasional words distinguishable |
| 3 | Readable with considerable difficulty |
| 4 | Readable with practically no difficulty |
| 5 | Perfectly readable |

| S | Strength |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Faint signal, barely perceptible |
| 2 | Very weak |
| 3 | Weak |
| 4 | Fair |
| 5 | Fairly good |
| 6 | Good |
| 7 | Moderately strong |
| 8 | Strong |
| 9 | Very strong signals |

| T | Tone (Morse only) |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Extremely rough hissing note |
| 2 | Very rough AC note, no trace of musicality |
| 3 | Rough AC tone, rectified but not filtered |
| 4 | Rough note, some trace of filtering |
| 5 | Filtered rectified AC but strongly ripple-modulated |
| 6 | Filtered tone, definite trace of ripple modulation |
| 7 | Near pure tone, trace of ripple modulation |
| 8 | Near perfect tone, slight trace of modulation |
| 9 | Perfect tone, no trace of ripple or modulation of any kind |

The guide adds that most operators now report the strength shown on the receiver's S-meter, with signals above S9 given in decibels ("five nine plus ten"), and that in Morse the figure 9 is often sent as the letter N, so 599 becomes 5NN.

## Band names

The official names for parts of the radio spectrum. Each band runs from above the lower figure up to and including the higher one.

| Symbol | Name | Frequency range | Wavelength |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| VLF | Very low frequency | 3 to 30 kHz | 100 km to 10 km |
| LF | Low frequency | 30 to 300 kHz | 10 km to 1 km |
| MF | Medium frequency | 300 to 3000 kHz | 1 km to 100 m |
| HF | High frequency | 3 to 30 MHz | 100 m to 10 m |
| VHF | Very high frequency | 30 to 300 MHz | 10 m to 1 m |
| UHF | Ultra high frequency | 300 to 3000 MHz | 1 m to 10 cm |
| SHF | Super high frequency | 3 to 30 GHz | 10 cm to 1 cm |
| EHF | Extremely high frequency | 30 to 300 GHz | 1 cm to 1 mm |

Frequencies are written in kilohertz (kHz) up to 3000 kHz, in megahertz (MHz) from there to 3000 MHz, and in gigahertz (GHz) above that. 1 MHz is 1000 kHz and 1 GHz is 1000 MHz.

## Frequency and wavelength

Wavelength in metres is 300 divided by the frequency in MHz (more exactly 299.792458, the speed of light in millions of metres per second). The same sum turns a wavelength back into a frequency.

| Frequency | Wavelength | Amateur band name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.9 MHz | 158 m | 160 m |
| 3.65 MHz | 82 m | 80 m |
| 7.1 MHz | 42 m | 40 m |
| 14.2 MHz | 21 m | 20 m |
| 21.2 MHz | 14 m | 15 m |
| 28.5 MHz | 10.5 m | 10 m |
| 50 MHz | 6 m | 6 m |
| 145 MHz | 2.07 m | 2 m |
| 435 MHz | 69 cm | 70 cm |

## Time

Radio schedules and logs are normally kept in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), which does not change with the seasons. UK local time is the same as UTC in winter and one hour ahead in summer. The UK's NAVTEX schedule, for example, is published in UTC, while HM Coastguard's voice broadcasts and the BBC's Shipping Forecast times are given in local time.

## Not covered

- The full Q code (several hundred entries) and the Z code.
- Accented letters in Morse, and Morse for other alphabets.
- Voice procedure words (for example "over" and "out"), distress procedure (MAYDAY, PAN-PAN), and the ten-codes used on some CB and public safety channels.
- Signal reports for digital modes, which use measured signal-to-noise figures instead of RST.
- The written-out names of the bands (such as "very high frequency") are the usual English names; the regulation read gives only the symbols.
- Antenna lengths and antenna design.
