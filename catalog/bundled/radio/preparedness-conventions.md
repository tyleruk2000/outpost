# Preparedness channel conventions

The informal radio conventions that preparedness groups pass around: the "3-3-3" plan, "channel 3", the Wilderness Protocol and the amateur calling frequencies. This sheet says where each one comes from and what it actually asks you to do.

## Read this first

These are unofficial conventions. None of them is a standard, none has any legal standing, and none comes with a guaranteed listener. No regulator, emergency service or rescue organisation monitors these channels because of these plans. They are agreements among hobbyists and local groups, published on blogs and in newsletters, and different groups follow different versions.

Their value is local and arranged in advance: a plan works when the people you want to reach have agreed to it and practised it. As a way of calling strangers for help, treat them as a last hope, not a plan.

## The law in brief

A convention gives no permission to transmit. Each channel belongs to a radio service with its own rules: transmitting needs the right licence unless the service is licence-exempt in the country you are in, and then only with equipment made for that service. FRS, GMRS and MURS are United States services and their radios must not be used in the UK; the UK and European equivalents are PMR446 and CB. The 2 metre frequencies need an amateur licence everywhere. Listening to amateur and CB traffic is lawful in the UK and the United States. This sheet is not legal advice.

## The conventions, and where they come from

| Convention | Where it comes from | What it says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The 3-3-3 radio plan | A post on the hobbyist blog Radio Free Q, 15 July 2013, which bases it on the survival "rule of three" | Turn your radio on every 3 hours, for at least 3 minutes, on channel 3 |
| Channel 3, or "3-2-1" | The CH3 Project of AmRRON, a private United States group that describes itself as "a nationwide emergency communications network for preppers and patriots" | Set the radio to channel 3, call for about 2 minutes, and try again every 1 hour, on the hour |
| The Wilderness Protocol | Proposed by a radio amateur, William Alsup (N6XMW), in the magazine QST in February 1994, and described in the ARRL's ARES E-Letter of 21 December 2005 | Radio amateurs in the back country listen on 146.520 MHz every three hours from 7 am to 7 pm local time, for five minutes |
| Calling frequencies | The voluntary band plans of the International Amateur Radio Union | Licensed amateurs make a first call on the calling frequency, then move elsewhere |

## The 3-3-3 plan in detail

| Part | What the 2013 post says |
| --- | --- |
| When | Every three hours, at the top of the hour, in local time: midnight, 3 am, 6 am, 9 am, noon, 3 pm, 6 pm and 9 pm |
| How long | At least three minutes |
| Where | Channel 3: "CB-3, FRS-3, or MURS-3". A group with its own agreed channel should use that instead |
| Why | The post lists, among other things, that it is easy to remember, conserves battery life and gets everyone on the air at the same time |

## AmRRON's channel 3 in detail

| Radio type | Channel | Frequency given by AmRRON |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CB | 3 | 26.985 MHz |
| FRS or GMRS | 3 | 462.6125 MHz |
| MURS | 3 | 151.940 MHz |

AmRRON adds: do not use sub-channels or "privacy" codes. Because its plan calls every hour and the 3-3-3 plan every third hour, the two meet at midnight, 3, 6 and 9 o'clock.

## The Wilderness Protocol in detail

This one is for licensed radio amateurs, on amateur simplex frequencies.

| Part | What the 2005 ARRL newsletter says |
| --- | --- |
| Primary frequency | 146.520 MHz |
| Other frequencies | 52.525, 223.500, 446.000 and 1294.500 MHz |
| When | Every three hours on the hour, from 7 am to 7 pm local time |
| How long | Five minutes; those with the time or a scanner are encouraged to listen more often |

These are United States frequencies. In the UK and the rest of Europe the 2 metre amateur band stops at 146 MHz, so 146.520 MHz is outside the amateur band there and must not be used. The nearest European equivalent is the Region 1 FM calling frequency, 145.500 MHz.

## What "channel 3" means on each kind of radio

| Service | Channel 3 | Where that service may be used without an amateur licence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CB, CEPT (EU) and United States channels | 26.985 MHz | UK, Europe and the United States, with a CB radio that meets the local rules |
| CB, UK-only band | 27.62125 MHz | UK only |
| PMR446 | 446.03125 MHz | UK and Europe, with a PMR446 radio |
| FRS (and GMRS with a licence) | 462.6125 MHz | United States only |
| MURS | 151.940 MHz, the third of the five MURS frequencies | United States only |

The MURS rules name frequencies, not channel numbers, so "MURS 3" is itself a convention. The 2013 post gives "PMR466-CH3" (that is, PMR446 channel 3) as one example of a channel a group might agree on instead.

## Amateur calling frequencies often named in plans

| Frequency | Band plan | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 145.500 MHz | IARU Region 1 (includes the UK) | FM calling |
| 146.520 MHz | IARU Region 2 (includes the United States) | FM calling |
| 3760 kHz and 7110 kHz | IARU Region 1 | Emergency centres of activity |
| 14300 kHz | All three IARU regions | Global emergency centre of activity |

All of these need an amateur licence. The band plans are voluntary too, and there is no promise that anyone is listening.

## The one channel that is in a rulebook

In the United States, the FCC rules say CB channel 9 (27.065 MHz) "may be used only for emergency communications or traveler assistance". That is a real rule about what the channel is for. It is still not a promise that anyone monitors it. The UK rules read for this sheet reserve no CB or PMR446 channel for emergencies.

## Using a convention sensibly

- Agree the plan with the people you actually want to reach, and say which version you mean: the 3-3-3 plan and AmRRON's plan are not the same.
- Write down the frequency as well as the channel number. Channel numbers differ between services and sometimes between radios.
- Agree whose clock you are using. The 3-3-3 plan uses local time.
- Assume anyone can hear you. These channels are published, and tone squelch gives no privacy.
- Keep within the service's rules for power, antenna and equipment. A radio that can be tuned to a channel is not thereby legal on it.
