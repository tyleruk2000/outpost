# Amateur radio bands worldwide

How the amateur bands differ around the world: the three regions used by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) and the International Amateur Radio Union (IARU), the international allocations from 135 kHz to 1300 MHz, and how the United Kingdom and the United States compare.

## The law in brief

An international allocation is not a permission. Each country decides which bands its amateurs may use, at what power and with which licence, and transmitting always needs that country's licence or a licence it recognises. Treat this sheet as a map of what to expect, then check the national rules. It is not legal advice.

## The three regions

| Region | Area |
| --- | --- |
| Region 1 | Africa, Europe, the Middle East and northern Asia |
| Region 2 | The Americas |
| Region 3 | The rest of Asia and the Pacific |

The exact boundaries are lines of latitude and longitude defined in the ITU Radio Regulations. The three IARU regional organisations cover the same areas.

## International allocations, 135 kHz to 30 MHz

"Primary" means the amateur service has full standing in the band (sometimes shared with other primary services). "Secondary" means amateurs must not cause interference to primary services and must accept interference from them.

Where the regions differ:

- 160 m. Region 1 has only 1810 to 1850 kHz in the international table. Many Region 1 countries add more under their own rules: the UK licence, for example, runs to 2000 kHz, with 1850 to 2000 kHz secondary.
- 80 m. The top of the band is 3800 kHz in Region 1, 3900 kHz in Region 3 and 4000 kHz in Region 2.
- 60 m. The international band is narrow and power is limited by the Radio Regulations. National rules vary a great deal: the UK uses eleven separate segments between 5258.5 and 5406.5 kHz, and the United States uses the international band plus four channels.
- 40 m. 7200 to 7300 kHz is amateur only in Region 2. In Regions 1 and 3 it is a broadcasting band.

## International allocations, 50 MHz to 1300 MHz

| Band | Region 1 | Region 2 | Region 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 m | 50 to 52 MHz, secondary (in force from 2021) | 50 to 54 MHz, primary | 50 to 54 MHz, primary |
| 2 m | 144 to 146 MHz, primary | 144 to 148 MHz, primary | 144 to 148 MHz, primary |
| 1.25 m | None | 220 to 225 MHz, primary | None |
| 70 cm | 430 to 440 MHz, primary | 430 to 440 MHz, secondary | 430 to 440 MHz, secondary |
| 33 cm | None | 902 to 928 MHz, secondary | None |
| 23 cm | 1240 to 1300 MHz, secondary | 1240 to 1300 MHz, secondary | 1240 to 1300 MHz, secondary |

Where the regions differ:

- 6 m. Regions 2 and 3 have 50 to 54 MHz. In Region 1 the general allocation is 50 to 52 MHz, secondary, and footnotes give many countries a primary allocation in all or part of 50 to 54 MHz: the UK licence gives 50 to 51 MHz as primary and 51 to 52 MHz as secondary.
- 4 m. There is no worldwide 70 MHz allocation. The UK licence includes 70.0 to 70.5 MHz as a secondary band.
- 2 m. 144 to 146 MHz is amateur everywhere. 146 to 148 MHz is amateur in Regions 2 and 3 only; in Region 1 it belongs to other services.
- 1.25 m and 33 cm exist only in Region 2. In the United States 219 to 220 MHz is also available for one restricted use, and the amateur band is 222 to 225 MHz.
- 70 cm. 430 to 440 MHz is the common part. The United States allows 420 to 450 MHz; the UK allows 430 to 440 MHz, all of it secondary.

## Emergency centres of activity

The IARU regions have agreed these frequencies as a focus for emergency communications. IARU Region 1 says they "are not 'absolute' frequencies but instead are 'centres of activity'", and that emergency traffic may be found within 20 kHz either side. They are voluntary: nobody is required to keep them clear or to listen on them.

| Band | Region 1 | Region 2 | Region 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 80 m | 3760 kHz | 3750 or 3985 kHz | 3600 kHz |
| 40 m | 7110 kHz | 7060, 7240 or 7275 kHz | 7110 kHz |
| 20 m | 14300 kHz | 14300 kHz | 14300 kHz |
| 17 m | 18160 kHz | 18160 kHz | 18160 kHz |
| 15 m | 21360 kHz | 21360 kHz | 21360 kHz |

## Not covered

- The ITU Radio Regulations themselves were not read for this sheet; the international table is taken from the IARU's 2020 summary of it. Changes made by the 2023 World Radiocommunication Conference, if any affect these bands, are not reflected.
- Footnotes to the international table, which give individual countries extra or different allocations.
- Bands above 1300 MHz, and the amateur-satellite service.
- Any country's rules other than the UK and the United States.
- Operating abroad under CEPT or reciprocal licences.
