# Celestial navigation

**Tools → Celestial navigation** works out where you are from sextant sights of the Sun, the
Moon, the planets and the 57 navigational stars, with no GPS, printed almanac, tables or
internet.

**It is for learning, and as a fallback**, not for navigating a vessel: use proper charts, a
published almanac and the means of navigation the law and good seamanship require. Never
look at the Sun through a sextant without its shades.

## Sights and fix

1. **Choose the position worked from**: your dead-reckoning estimate, a saved place, or
   Home. Tens of miles out is fine; the fix is worked again from the answer until it
   settles.
2. **Stars to shoot now** suggests up to three bright stars between 15° and 70° up, spread
   round the sky. Star sights need the stars and the horizon together, in twilight.
3. **For each sight**, give the body (and the limb, for the Sun or Moon), the time in **UT**
   to the second, the sextant altitude (`34 12.5` or decimal degrees), the index error in
   minutes (**+ if the sextant reads high**), the horizon (sea, an artificial horizon, which
   doubles the angle, or a plumb line), and for the sea your height of eye in metres.
4. Each sight shows every correction and the reduction step by step, so you can check it by
   hand, ending in the intercept, towards or away.
5. **The fix.** One sight gives a line of position; two or more that cross give a fix, with
   the ellipse it lies in nineteen times in twenty. The screen warns when lines cross at a
   narrow angle or one sight disagrees.
6. **Running fix.** If you moved between sights, give the course and speed made good; the fix
   is for the time of the last sight. Two Sun sights a few hours apart make a fix by day.

The fix is drawn on a plotting sheet wide enough for every line, however far it passes from
the position worked from; **Show on the map** puts the lines, fix and ellipse on
the offline map. Sights are kept on this device.

## Noon and Polaris

- **Latitude from the Sun at noon.** Follow the lower limb up until it stops rising and read
  the highest altitude, with its time and whether the Sun bore south or north. The longitude
  from the time of noon is shown too, but rough: a minute's doubt is a quarter of a degree.
- **Latitude from Polaris**, north of the equator: give the time and altitude.

## Without a sextant

A protractor or home-made quadrant with a plumb line measures from the vertical, so there is
no horizon or dip; choose the plumb-line horizon. Read to half a degree, a line is good to
about 30 miles; to a degree, 60. Never sight the Sun along it: let it through a pinhole on to
the card.

## The clock

Four seconds of clock error is a minute of longitude, a nautical mile at the equator. Set
your watch by a radio time signal (**Radio → Frequencies**) and check the server's clock on
**Settings → Server status**.

**Save** gives a printable form for three sights, with the formulas and a worked Sun sight.

## How accurate it is

Against the US Naval Observatory's data for 2026 to 2035: stars, Aries and the Sun within
0.5′ (half a nautical mile); the Moon and planets within 2′. Trust star and Sun sights most.
Left out: the Moon's augmentation (a few tenths of a minute), the air's temperature and
pressure (refraction is for average air, least certain below 10°), and clock error.
