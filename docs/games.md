# Games

Small games for when the internet is out, under **Games** in the navigation, in the
phone's More sheet and in the quick search. None of them reaches the internet; there is
no sound.

Allotment, Steading, Solitaire, Peg solitaire, Sudoku, Draughts, Chess, Pairs, Snake and
Skerry.

## What is kept, and where

- **The allotment and the Steading** are kept on the server, one for the household.
  **Start again** at the foot of each clears it for everyone, after asking.
- **Each game's tally** (played, won, best time, fewest moves) is on the server, shared
  by the household. **Clear the tally** under the board forgets it, after asking.
- **An unfinished game**, Snake's scores and Skerry's progress stay in the browser they
  are played in, so two people on two devices never overwrite each other. In a private
  window a game plays the same and is not kept.

Games are in every backup and removed by erase everything.

## Allotment

A small plot of beds. Sow seeds, water them, harvest, take the produce to the stall for
coins, and buy seeds, more beds and a few lasting things. It is also a small almanac of
real growing in Britain.

**Nothing is ever lost.** Nothing withers or dies: a crop out of its months, or in dry
soil, waits with all its growth; a ripe crop waits for ever. Radish seed and onion sets
are free, so the plot is never stuck. Eggs, honey and apples stop at a small cap while
you are away. There are no streaks, rewards, notifications or ending.

### The rules

A crop grows only while it is **in its months** (from its earliest sowing to its last
harvest in Britain; under glass, every month) **and its bed is moist**:

- for 12 hours after watering with the can (watering again tops it up);
- in the open, all through a wet day and 12 hours after it;
- in the open with the **water butt**, all through a wet day and 2 days after it.

No rain falls under glass and the butt does not reach the greenhouse: there, only the
can waters. Whether a day is wet comes from the date, so it is the same on every device:
30% of days from April to July, rising to 50% in November and December (northern months).

The season is the real month. The hemisphere is northern unless Home (the saved place
with the home icon) is south of the equator; the switch on the screen overrides it. In
the south, the months, the crops' months and the rain move on six.

### The crops

The months a crop grows in come from the Royal Horticultural Society's "grow your own"
pages ([sources.md](sources.md#the-allotments-months-and-facts-read-2026-11-17)); each
crop's card in the game gives its sowing and harvest months. Growing time (in moist soil,
in its months), prices and yields are made up.

| Crop | Grows (in the open) | Growing time | Seed | Yield | Sells each |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Radishes | March to October | 4 minutes | free | 3 | 1 |
| Salad leaves | February to December | 25 minutes | 4 | 4 | 2 |
| Onions | all year | 1 hour | free | 3 | 2 |
| Peas | February to October | 2 hours | 10 | 5 | 4 |
| Carrots | February to November | 6 hours | 15 | 6 | 5 |
| Beetroot | March to October | 12 hours | 20 | 5 | 8 |
| Kale | all year | 18 hours | 20 | 6 | 7 |
| Broad beans | October to August | 1 day | 25 | 6 | 9 |
| Potatoes | March to October | 1 day | 30 | 8 | 7 |
| Leeks | all year | 2 days | 40 | 5 | 18 |
| Garlic | October to August | 2½ days | 35 | 6 | 16 |
| Squash | April to October | 3 days | 60 | 3 | 45 |

You start with 10 coins and 4 beds. The next bed costs 25 × (beds − 3) coins, up to 12
beds.

| Lasting thing | Costs | Does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water butt | 40 | Keeps the open beds watered for 2 days after each wet day. |
| Compost bin | 80 | Each harvest adds 1 compost (at most 8); a sowing uses one, and that bed yields 2 more. |
| Hens | 120 | An egg every 3 hours, at most 6 waiting; 3 coins each. |
| Beehive | 200 | A jar of honey every 8 hours, at most 4 waiting; 12 coins each. |
| Apple tree | 300 | An apple every 6 hours, at most 12 waiting; 3 coins each. |
| Greenhouse | 500 | Two more beds under glass: every crop grows all year, but no rain falls. |

Coins stop at 999,999 and each kind in the basket at 9,999; a harvest the basket cannot
hold stays in its bed.

### Reading the plot

- A bed's label shows the time left, **Ripe**, or why it waits ("Dry", "Till Mar"). Tap a
  bed for what would change it.
- **This month on the plot** says what to sow now and what is waiting for what. Sowing
  out of season is never refused; the list says first what will happen.
- **The crops** has a card per crop: its months, how it takes cold and dry, and, once
  grown, its family and a few facts.

How long water lasts and how often it rains are made up too. It is not a planting guide
for any one garden: the southern months are Britain's moved on six.

## Steading

A settlement for the household to build, on a kind of land you choose. Everything is
made from something and carried from somewhere: a bakery with no flour bakes nothing, and
a quarry with no route or belt fills its yard and stops.

**Leave it as long as you like: nothing here ever gets worse while you are away.** That
line is on the game's page. Work goes on while it is closed until the stores and yards
are full, then stops, with everything kept. Nothing decays, leaves or is used up by
itself; there are no streaks, timers, reminders or events. The only things that go down
while nobody plays are the charge in the batteries and the diesel in a generator's tank,
which are there to be used: a battery that runs flat at night only slows the machines
until the sun or wind is back, and nothing breaks or is lost. Coming back there is
nothing to catch up on.

### Choosing the land

A new settlement (and one after **Start again**) begins by choosing the land and the
seed. The map, 48 squares each way, is drawn up from them: the same land and seed draw
the same map on any Outpost, so a seed can be read out to share a map. **Another seed**
draws a new one; the seed is shown under the game's heading all the time.

Each land is drawn from a real place, and its sun, wind and cold are that place's:

| Land | Drawn from | A 4.8 kW array, June / December | A 10 kW turbine, a day on average | Firewood against the river valley | Grain against the river valley | Also |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| River valley | Keswick, Cumbria | 17 / 2.6 kWh a day | 13 kWh | 100% | 100% | no ore: it comes by barge |
| Mountains | Davos, Switzerland (1,551 m) | 20 / 2.4 | 0 | 167% | 31% | rock, coal and ore close by; houses need 8 more bricks |
| Snow and tundra | Kiruna, Sweden | 19 / 0 | 2 | 167% | 52% | lakes, few trees, ore; houses need 10 more bricks |
| Desert | Ouarzazate, Morocco | 24 / 24 | 6 | 55% | 150% | wells draw 400 litres, not 1,000; fields within 2 squares of water; trees only by the river |
| Coast | Belmullet, Ireland | 19 / 3.6 | 68 | 78% | 137% | the sea along the west; little rock |
| Forest | Freudenstadt, Black Forest | 20 / 6.6 | 3 | 98% | 142% | woods everywhere; houses need 4 more bricks |

Every map has woods, rock and clay within a few squares of where the settlement is
founded, so a new one is never stuck. Coal and ore lie only where the land has them.

The firewood a household burns follows the cold, and a field's harvest the warmth; those
two are the game's rules on real figures (below). The water a well draws, the extra
bricks and where fields may stand are made up for the game.

A settlement founded before lands could be chosen keeps Ashgill, its small valley of 10
by 14 squares, with everything in it as it was; it gains belts and power like any other.

### The map

The map is a window on the land: drag to move it, pinch or scroll to zoom, or use
**Zoom in**, **Zoom out** and **Back to the storehouse**. With the keyboard, Tab to the
plan, move with the arrow keys (the window follows), + and − to zoom, and Enter to choose
a square. Every square in view is a button with a spoken name ("Column 24, row 18,
Woodcutter, working", "Column 25, row 18, roller belt heading east, carrying 24 a
day"). Squares are small on a phone: every building is also in the list beside the plan.

### Production chains

- Choose a square of open ground to build on it. Each building shows what it uses and
  makes in a day, its workers and its cost. Woodcutters, quarries, clay pits, coal pits
  and ore mines stand beside their woods, rock, clay, coal or ore; the water wheel and
  the waterworks beside the river; the landing beside the river or the shore.
  Woodcutters, quarries, clay pits and fields are free, so the settlement is never stuck.
- A building sends what it makes along **routes**: to a workshop that uses it, or to a
  storehouse. Choose the building, then **Send its … to…**, the place and how it is
  carried:

  | Carried | Per day | Reach | Costs a square |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | By hand | 12 | 3 squares | nothing |
  | Cart road | 40 | 8 squares | a plank and a stone |
  | Track (40 people) | 160 | 20 squares | a bar of iron and a plank |

- Or along **belts**, laid square by square (below).
- A building fills its first route first, then its belts; **Fill this first** changes the
  order of routes. What cannot be sent on waits in its yard (20 of each thing); a full
  yard rests the work. **Carry the yard to the store** empties it by hand.
- The chains: logs to a sawmill (planks) or a woodyard (firewood); clay and firewood to a
  brick kiln; grain to a mill, flour and firewood to a bakery; ore (mined, or by barge)
  and coal to a smelter, iron and planks to a smithy.
- Each building says what holds it back: short of workers, waiting for an input, or its
  yard full. A flag on its corner marks it on the plan. The plan draws every route as a
  line whose width is what it carries in a day, dashed by hand, solid for a cart road,
  railed for track.
- The **ledger** lists every good: what is in store (each storehouse holds 200 of each),
  what is kept each day, what goes to the households, and how much has been made.
  Workshops take only what is sent to them, which is why time never draws a stock down.
- Taking a building down gives back everything it and its routes cost, its yard and the
  diesel in its tank. A house with a household in it stays.

### Belts

Choose a square of open ground and **Lay a belt from here**, then choose squares on the
plan one after another: each carries on from the last, and a square further along the
same row or column fills in the straight run (an L to any other). Choosing a building
next to the end turns the end into it. **Turn the last square** sets its way (north,
east, south, west, or a junction); **Undo last** and **Cancel** are beside **Lay**.

- A belt takes from the building its first square leads away from, and carries into the
  building its last square leads into, if that building uses what is sent (or is a
  storehouse) and is a later step of the chain.
- Each square carries so much in a day: a **roller belt** 60 (a plank a square), a **chain
  belt** 240 (a plank and a bar of iron a square, once 24 people live here). Where belts
  meet they merge and share the squares after; a **junction** (twice the cost) lets two
  belts cross, each going straight on, each way carrying the full amount.
- A belt that runs into nothing, off the land, or round in a loop carries nothing.
- On the plan a belt is a band with its way marked; what it carries runs along it, faster
  the more it carries (still with reduced motion). A belt square's panel says what it
  carries, which line runs through it, and turns it, makes it a chain belt or takes it up
  (giving back what it cost).
- A settlement keeps at most 600 squares of belt.

### Households

A new settlement has a storehouse, two households of four and a well. People work within
4 squares of home. A new house fills once it has a well within 3 squares, a storehouse
within 5, and enough water, bread and firewood coming in for it as well as for everyone
already there; otherwise it says what it waits for. Households never leave, and a
shortage only keeps a new house waiting.

What a household of four uses in a day, from real figures:

| | A day | From |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food | 9,000 kcal, about 4.2 loaves of 800 g | 2,250 kcal a person (Public Health England); 266 kcal in 100 g of white bread (USDA) |
| Water | 60 litres | 15 litres a person, the top of WHO's 7.5 to 15 for drinking, cooking and basic hygiene |
| Heat | 26 kWh, about 6.3 kg of dry logs, in the river valley | 9,500 kWh of gas a year for a typical household (Ofgem, from July 2026); 4.1 kWh per kg of air-dry logs (Forest Research) |
| Electricity | 6.8 kWh | 2,500 kWh a year for a typical household (Ofgem) |

The heat is what a typical household's gas gives, counted as if from wood; a stove and a
boiler are not equally efficient, and the game does not count the difference. On another
land two thirds of it goes up and down with the land's heating degree days (base
15.5 °C) against the river valley's; the third for hot water and cooking stays the same.
That split is the game's rule.

### Power

Once 24 people live here, power can be built. Every source and battery is on one line
for the whole settlement. Houses use it for light (it is not needed to move in); a
sawmill, mill or smithy runs twice as fast on 40 kWh a day.

| Building | What it is | Makes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water wheel | beside the river | 120 kWh a day, day and night (made up for the game) |
| Solar array | twelve 400 W panels, 4.8 kW peak | the land's sun for the month, in daylight only |
| Wind turbine | a 10 kW turbine with a 7 m rotor; may stand on a fell | the land's wind for the month, day and night |
| Generator | 10 kW on diesel, with a 200-litre tank | only when the batteries are at their floor |
| Lead-acid battery | 24 cells of 2 V 1,000 Ah: 48 V, 1,000 Ah, 48 kWh | 50% of it used, 80% round trip |
| Lithium battery | 16 LiFePO4 cells of 3.2 V 280 Ah: 51.2 V, 280 Ah, 14.3 kWh | 80% of it used, 92% round trip |

In a moment, the sun, the wind and the wheel come first. What they make beyond the load
charges the batteries, each way losing the square root of the round trip; what they fall
short of comes from the batteries, down to the floor they are kept above. Only then does
a generator start: it follows the load and does not charge the batteries, burning 0.0331
litres an hour for each kW of its size while it runs and 0.2729 litres for each kWh it
makes (about 73 litres a day at full load). When everything together falls short, the
machines go without first, then the lights. Charge and discharge current limits are not
counted.

A settlement day passes in an hour, so the sun rises and sets each hour: an array makes
its day's energy evenly through the land's daylight hours for the month (a real panel
makes most at noon). The month is the calendar's. The **Power** section says what is made
and wanted now, the batteries in amp-hours, kilowatt-hours and how full they are, each
generator's tank with **Fill the tank from the store** (diesel comes in 20-litre cans, from
the barge or a landing trade), and, behind a disclosure, the land's month by month and
where every figure comes from.

Panels, battery cells and diesel come by barge: the landing sells them for credit, and
can trade bricks for diesel every day.

### Trade

Once 12 people live here a **landing** on the river or the shore can be built. Choose a
trade for it (planks, bricks or bread for ore; grain for coal; bricks for diesel) and lay
routes or belts to it and from it: the barge then takes and brings that every day. The
landing also buys anything from the store at a fixed price, for credit, and sells coal,
ore, iron, tools, diesel, solar panels and battery cells for credit. Prices never change.

### Time

A day in the settlement passes in an hour, and every rate is per such day. Between the
moments when something changes (a store or yard fills, the batteries fill or reach their
floor, a tank runs dry, the sun rises or sets, the month turns, a household moves in)
every flow is steady, so the server works the settlement out from the time exactly,
however long it was left; within a month each day is the same as the last, so whole days
are carried forward at once. There is no timer on the server. Each action is applied
with the server's clock, and a clock that jumps back changes nothing. **Start again** at
the foot of the screen clears the settlement for a new one, for everyone, and asks first.

A settlement kept before lands, belts and batteries came (version 1) is read as version
2 when it is next opened: the same buildings, routes, stock and milestones on Ashgill,
with no belts and empty batteries.

What is real and what is made up: each land's sun, wind and cold, the turbine's power
curve, the batteries' voltages, usable share and round trip, the generator's fuel line,
and the household figures are real (sources in [sources.md](sources.md#the-steadings-lands-and-power-read-2026-11-21)).
Every rate of work, cost, reach and price, the belts, the water wheel's output, the
sizes of a loaf and a bundle of firewood, how heat and harvest follow the land, and the
hour-long day are made up for the game.

### Size and speed

The Steading adds 111 KB of script (38 KB compressed), loaded when the screen is opened,
and no image, font or sound files: 51 KB (18 KB compressed) more than before lands,
belts and power, almost all of it the lands' monthly figures, the map generator and the
new panels. A map is not kept; it is drawn again from its seed in a few milliseconds.

Measured in Chromium at a phone's size (390 by 844) with the processor slowed six times
(a stand-in for a phone; not yet tried on a real one): the screen opens with the
settlement in 1.1 seconds; dragging the map holds 60 frames a second (the slowest frame
67 ms, as the drag starts); zooming out to the whole 48 by 48 map takes one frame of
about 0.2 seconds (it then has 2,304 squares to name), after which dragging is smooth; a
tap opens a square's panel in under 0.2 seconds. A year away is worked out in well under
a second.

## Solitaire

Klondike, draw one or draw three. A tapped card goes to its best place (its foundation,
else the next pile it fits on, else an empty pile); the setting under the board switches
to pick and put, and cards can be dragged. **Put away all that can go** sends every card
it can to the foundations and offers to finish when that is possible. Undo takes back
every move; time counts only while the tab is visible.

## Peg solitaire

The English board of 33 holes, all filled but the centre (d4). Jump a peg along a row or
column over a neighbour into an empty hole; the jumped peg is removed. Tap a peg, then a
marked hole, or drag it. One peg left is solved, best of all in the centre. The tally
keeps the fewest pegs left in a game played out.

## Sudoku

Each puzzle is made on the device and has exactly one answer. **Gentle** (38 givens) and
**Steady** (30) need only singles; **Testing** has as few givens as one answer allows and
needs more. Pencil notes, undo, erase, and **Check**, which marks wrong cells without
showing an answer. The number pad shows how many of each digit are left; the keyboard
works too. On a narrow phone the cells are smaller than Outpost's usual 44 px so that
nine fit across; the pad's keys are full size.

## Draughts

English draughts, the rules shown under the board: captures are compulsory, kings move
one square either way (not flying kings), and 40 moves each with no capture and no man
moved is a draw. Against the computer, play dark or light; it looks 3 moves each ahead.
Undo takes back a whole turn.

## Chess

Chess by the FIDE Laws (in force from 1 January 2023), against the computer or two at
one device, the rules under the board. Every rule is played, including castling, en
passant and promotion to any piece. Threefold repetition and the 50 moves end the game
at once rather than waiting for a claim; dead positions other than insufficient material
(such as locked pawn chains) are not spotted.

Tap a piece to see its legal squares, then tap one or drag. **Turn the board** shows it
from the other side. **Copy the game** gives PGN and FEN; **Paste a game** reads either.

The computer is a small search of Outpost's own, with no opening book, endgame tables or
chess engine:

| Level | Looks ahead | Plays like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learner | 1 ply | Blunders often: a child who knows the moves can beat it. |
| Easy | 2 plies | Takes what is left hanging, misses most plans. |
| Medium | 3 plies | A steady opponent for a casual player. |
| Hard | 4 plies | A fair game for a casual player; any club player or chess program will beat it. |

None has been rated against people. On a Raspberry Pi or an old phone it replies more
slowly.

## Pairs

Turn over two cards at a time; a match stays up, a miss turns back. Small is 6 pairs,
medium 8, large 12. The pictures are things a small child can name. No clock is shown
while playing.

## Snake

Steer a snake to the apples on a 16 by 16 field; each apple adds a cell and a point. It
ends when the head hits the body, or the edge unless **Wrap round** is on. Filling the
field wins.

Steer with the arrow keys, W A S D, a swipe or the buttons under the field. Four speeds,
from slow (for small children) to fast. Space or P pauses; it pauses itself when the page
is hidden.

## Skerry

You are the relief keeper of the weather station on Tarrow Skerry, a made-up island. The
last keeper, Ines, left her notes in the station computer, which is worked by typing
commands. Each of the twelve levels asks one question, and the files hold the answer.

- Type a command and press Enter. `help` lists commands, `man <command>` explains one,
  `goal` repeats the question, and `answer` gives yours (`answer 5`). Some levels are
  solved by reading the right file.
- `hint` gives a hint, as many as you like; the last one all but says what to type.
- The commands behave like the real ones (`ls`, `cd`, `cat`, `grep`, `sort`, `find` and
  others, joined with `|`); `decode 3 note.txt` undoes a Caesar shift of three.
- Tab completes names; the arrow keys bring back what you typed. On a phone, the buttons
  under the terminal do the same and type `|`. Play any level, in any order.

The command line is pretend: it reads only the level's made-up files, changes nothing,
runs nothing on the device or on Outpost, and reaches no network.
