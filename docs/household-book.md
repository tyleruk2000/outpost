# The household book

**Preparedness → Household book** asks a few questions about your household and makes a
printable book from the answers and from what Outpost already holds: your emergency plans
and a fridge sheet for each, contacts, first aid, water, frequencies, journeys, checklists,
grab cards and more. Print all of it, or only the sections you choose (only the fridge
sheets, say, or only the grab cards). It is made on the
device you press **Make the book** on, and saved, added to the library or shared like
every PDF Outpost makes.

## The questions

Six steps. Your answers are saved each time you move on (**Save and go on**), so you can
stop (**Save and stop here**) and come back on any device. **Change the answers** takes
you through them again.

1. **People and pets.** Everyone in the household, from **Preparedness → Households**
   ([Households](households.md)). A person added here is added there, since that is
   where the food and water they need is worked out.
   For each person you can say what they need ("uses a wheelchair") and carry ("inhaler
   in coat pocket"), and tick **A card of their own** for a child's card. Medical sheets
   are made under **Medical sheets**.
2. **Home.** The saved place with the home icon. With none, mark it on Maps, or type its
   latitude and longitude and press **Save as home**. The emergency number is filled in
   from the country home is in where Outpost knows it; type another to use that.
3. **Where you would go and meet.** Meeting points near home and outside the area, and
   the people you would go to. Each is a saved place on the map; a person can also be a
   contact, whose saved place then stands in for theirs.
4. **Vehicles and radios.** Each vehicle's range on a full tank or charge, and the kinds
   of radio set you have.
5. **How long, and which hazards.** The days to be ready for, the hours you would walk in
   a day on a long journey, and the hazards that matter to you. A hazard you have an
   emergency plan for starts ticked, and says which plan. A ticked hazard with no plan
   offers **Make a plan for this**: it opens **Emergency plans** with that template
   chosen, and **Back to the household book** brings you back to this step. Under **Your
   emergency plans**, choose whether all of them go in the book or only some.
6. **What goes in the book.** The household's name for the cover, the paper (A4 or US
   Letter, first set from the country home is in), the sections, blank pages for notes,
   whether each medical sheet goes in in full, and the size of the grab cards (wallet or
   A6). Untick sections to print only some. Under **Your radio plans**, choose whether
   every radio plan from **Radio → Plans** goes in, or only some. Radio plans are kept
   for every household, so each household's book takes all of them unless you choose.

## What is in the book

A cover with the number to call, what changed since the last book and what the book
cannot know; the contents; then each section you chose, numbered, starting on a page of
its own; then the sources. Every page has the book's name and its page number at the
foot.

| Section | Made from |
| --- | --- |
| The first few minutes | Official guidance on going in or getting out, your meeting points, check-in time and first contact |
| Fridge sheets | One page for each emergency plan in the book: the number, what to do, who to call, where to meet |
| Our household | Supplies' household, your answers, and the medical sheets that can be read on this device |
| Keeping in touch | Contacts marked for emergencies, and the communication plan, with a page for each person |
| Home and meeting points | A map of home and every meeting point from the offline map on this device, a drawing of where to go in each case, each place in several grid formats, and the people you would go to |
| First aid | Lines of the NHS website's pages, unchanged, and FEMA's words on the recovery position, with drawings of them |
| Help near home | Hospitals, pharmacies, emergency services, shelters, drinking water, food banks and shops in the reference layers, with distance and direction |
| Places to keep away from | Nuclear and military sites, airports and fuel stations in the reference layers, and the wind at home |
| What to do, by situation | Every emergency plan in the book, in full and as you wrote it; for a hazard with no plan, the template's general advice, marked as such |
| Water | How much for your people and days (with a drawing of each person's litres a day), how to make it safe (the steps drawn in order, then quoted, with drawings of a layered filter, catching rain and boiling), and drinking water near home |
| Food, toilet and hygiene | The energy your household needs, food after a power cut or a flood (with the hours for the fridge and the freezer drawn on a line), keeping clean with little water |
| Warmth, light and the radio | Official guidance on keeping warm and lit |
| Radio frequencies | The communication plan's channels first, then licence-free channels, emergency broadcasting, distress and calling, broadcasters your sets can hear, and repeaters near each of your places with a map of the ground each can reach |
| Radio plans | Each radio plan from **Radio → Plans** you chose: when to listen and call in local time and UTC, the bands best first, the stations, and the plan's day drawn |
| Journeys | Each place you would go to, on foot and by car, with a drawing of the route |
| Checklists | A shopping list for your people and days, the grab bag, the first aid kit and the car, with a box for **Got it** and **To get** |
| Supplies | What is kept, where and until when, and how long food and water last drawn against the days you want |
| Local risks | Flood and emergency planning zones at home where a layer is held, and the climate's extremes |
| Grab cards | A card for each person at its real size, wallet (folded) or A6, with marks to cut along: medical lines, who to call, where to meet, home's position |
| Cards for the children | A card each, to cut out |
| Pages for notes | Ruled pages |

Each emergency plan's steps are your own and are printed as your plan. Steps that came
from a template keep their source, listed at the back. **Emergency plans** and each plan
say whether it is in the book, with **Put in the household book** when the book takes
only some, and **Make the book again** when the book is older than the plans.

Something the book needs and Outpost does not hold is said where it belongs, with how to
get it: a reference layer (**Maps → Layers**), the climate for home (**Weather and
hazards → Climate**), terrain for the journeys, a communication plan.

## Radio frequencies and licences

Each frequency says in two words whether it needs a licence: **No licence**, **Listen
only** or **Licence needed**, each with its own mark so it reads on a black-and-white
copy. The rules behind them, and anything Outpost's list says about listening, are
numbered notes at the end of the section.

## Repeaters near your places

The book lists amateur radio repeaters from the lists you brought in under **Radio →
Repeaters**, round every place it covers: home (or, for a household on the move, where it
last was), each meeting point, the plans' places, the people you would go to, and along
the straight line of each journey. For each place it takes up to 6 (8 along a journey),
the nearest first: 2 m repeaters within 50 km, 70 cm within 40 km, 10 m, 6 m and 4 m
within 80 km, and 23 cm and above within 25 km. FM repeaters come before digital-only
ones, and those listed as not operational are left out. These distances are Outpost's
choice of what is worth trying, not a range.

Each repeater is listed once, under the first place it is near; a later place names it
as also near. The table gives the callsign, the output, the input with its shift, the
tone, the mode, where it is, its distance and direction from the place (from the line,
and how far along, for a journey), and its likely range. A short key under the first
table says what the columns mean: the output is the frequency you listen on (the repeater
transmits), the input the one you transmit on (the repeater listens), the shift is the
input minus the output, and the tone (CTCSS, or a DCS code) is what your radio must send
to open the repeater. Under the tables the book names each list the repeaters came from
and the date of its data.

A map follows for each place with repeaters of its own and for each journey, the full
width of the page: the offline map, the place, and the repeaters with their callsigns,
each label kept inside the frame and clear of the others. Round each repeater the map
shades the ground in sight of its aerial for a handheld at head height (1.5 m), worked
out from the terrain installed, as **Estimated radio range** on the map shades it. The
aerial is at the height the list gives, or 10 m where it gives none, and the caption
names the repeaters whose height was assumed. Each repeater's ground has a light tint, a
hatching at its own angle and an outline with its own dash, and the key under the map
shows each one, so overlapping areas read apart in colour and on a black-and-white copy.

The book screen says how far it has got ("Working out repeater coverage, 3 of 6"). Where no terrain is installed under a
repeater, a circle at its radio horizon over flat ground stands in, and the caption says
so. Either way it is line of sight only, a rough guide: buildings and trees block more,
and a signal often reaches a little past a ridge. The map is drawn from the offline map
on the device the book is made on; with none installed, or none covering the place, the
places and repeaters are drawn to scale on a plain grid with a scale bar and north.
Nothing is fetched from the internet to make it.

## Radio plans

**Radio plans** has a part for each radio plan from **Radio → Plans** that goes in the
book. It is its own section, not part of **Radio frequencies**: a radio plan is a
schedule between named stations on HF bands, worked out for a month, while the
frequencies are channels to listen to or talk on at any time.

Each part starts with **When to listen and call**: the plan's window, and for each pair
of stations the first and second band with the hours each serves, in UTC and in local
time. Local time is the time zone of the device the book is made on, on the plan's date,
so summer time is counted when the plan is for a summer month. A drawing of the plan's
day follows, an hour a cell, with UTC over it and local time under it. Then the bands in
order of preference (the plan's own table, then its other bands), the stations, each
pair's grid of hours on every band as the plan's own paper prints it, and the plan's
notes. A station shows a call sign when a contact marked for emergencies has the
station's name or call sign. An emergency plan that names the radio plan is listed.

The figures are the matrix last worked out for the plan, as it stands, on this Outpost.
A plan with none (never worked out, or changed since) goes in with its bands in the order
it lists them, and the book says to open it, press **Work out the plan**, and make the
book again.

An emergency plan's own **Keeping in touch** (its words, the frequency, when, and the
radio plan it names) is printed on its fridge sheet and with the plan in full, each part
named, with where the radio plan is in the book.

## Names in other scripts

The book's typeface has Latin letters only. A name in Greek, Cyrillic, Arabic, Hebrew,
Devanagari, Thai, Chinese, Japanese kana or Korean is set in a Noto Sans font, kept in
Outpost and loaded only when a book needs it. A rare Chinese or Korean character outside
the common set prints as an empty box. Arabic and Hebrew read right to left, with Arabic's
letters joined, in the text and in the drawings. Keep them to names and short phrases: a
right-to-left passage long enough to wrap reads its lines in the wrong order.

## Journeys

The book does not use road routes: each journey is the straight line from home, and the
book says so. For a drive, plan it under **Maps → Routes → Road route** and print its
[route card](road-routes.md#saving-and-printing) to keep with the book. The time on foot is worked out along that line with Tobler's hiking
function (5 km/h on level ground, slower up and down slopes), from the terrain installed:
for a fit adult with no load, and at half that pace for someone hurt, tired or with small
children. Where each day's walking ends follows from the hours a day you gave, and is
named after the nearest town in the reference layers. Without terrain the line is taken as
level. By car the book compares each vehicle's range with the straight line, and asks you
to keep a paper road map.

## The wind at home

The book reads the average direction the wind comes from at home from the climate
averages kept under **Weather and hazards** (NASA POWER). A site within 45° of that
direction is marked upwind. Averages fetched before Outpost asked for the wind's direction
have none: fetch them again.

## Where the words come from

Every piece of advice is quoted from a named publisher and listed at the back with the
date it was read. Official guidance is the emergency plan templates' (mostly the UK
government's Be Ready pages, under the Open Government Licence); a book for a home outside
the UK says so. First aid is whole lines of the NHS website's pages, unchanged and
credited as its terms ask. The recovery position is FEMA's words, which are free of
copyright. The NHS no longer has pages of its own on adult CPR, choking or severe
bleeding, and St John Ambulance, the British Red Cross and Resuscitation Council UK do not
allow theirs to be reprinted, so the book points to them instead. Clearing cloudy water
and catching rain are the CDC's and the US Army's survival manual's words. It holds no
guidance on toilets without water.

Each drawing shows what the words beside it say and no more. Where the words leave
something open (the US Army's filter names its materials but no order, and no bottle), the
caption says so.

## What changed since

Outpost keeps a few lines about each book it makes. The next book's cover lists what was
added or taken out in each section since. Make the book again whenever the household, its
plans or its places change, and replace the printed copy.

When anything the last book was made from has changed or been deleted since (the answers,
emergency plans, contacts, saved places, medical sheets, Keeping in touch, radio plans,
supplies and the household), Home and the book's screen say which, with a link to make it again. Facts read
round home (help nearby, the wind, terrain) are not watched. A household binder made
before the binder became part of the book stands in for the book until the book is made.

## Several households

Each household has its own answers and its own book, made for the current household. With
more than one, the cover and the foot of each page name the household: the name typed in
the questions, or the household's name when none was typed.

A household that moves is measured from where it was last said to be, set on its page
under **Preparedness → Households**. The book says where that was and when ("Where the van
was on 20 November 2026"), and how long before the book was made. On the cover it lists the
household's people and kit rather than an address, and a child's card says who the child is
travelling with. Set where it is, then make the book again, before relying on what it says
is near.

## What it does not do

- It does not know whether a place listed is open, stocked or still there.
- It does not follow roads or paths (a road route's card is printed separately).
- It does not give medical advice of its own, and checks no dose.
- It does not list power stations other than nuclear ones, chemical works or major hazard
  sites: Outpost holds no openly licensed copy of them.
- An encrypted medical sheet is in the book only once opened on this device, and is then
  in the clear on paper. Adding the book to the library asks first.
