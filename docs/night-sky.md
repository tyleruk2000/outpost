# Night sky

**Tools → Night sky** shows the Moon tonight, a chart of the stars and planets over a place
now or at another time, and how to find north (or south) by the stars. It is worked out on
the device from a star catalogue and the orbits of the Moon and planets that come with
Outpost, so it works with no internet.

## Using it

1. **Choose the place and the clock.** The home place (the saved place with the home icon)
   unless you choose another saved place or type coordinates. The place and the clock are
   the Solar clock's, remembered on this device.
2. **Choose when.** *Now* follows the device's clock and moves on each minute. *Another time*
   takes a date and a time on the chosen clock.

## The Moon

The phase, how much of the face is lit, and when the Moon rises and sets between midday and
the next midday, with the next new, first quarter, full and last quarter moons. Rising and
setting are for a level horizon: hills, trees and buildings make the Moon appear later and
vanish sooner.

## The chart

A round chart of the whole sky. The centre is straight overhead and the rim is the horizon.
North is at the top, and **east is on the left**: hold it over your head with the top
towards north and it matches the sky. Stars are dots, larger for brighter ones, down to
magnitude 4.5, which a dark country sky shows easily; a town sky shows far fewer. Twenty-seven
familiar constellations are joined up. Planets are filled dots in the accent colour; the
Moon is an open circle. The brightest stars and the planets are named where there is room.

Below the chart, **What is up** lists the Moon and the planets above the horizon and the ten
brightest named stars, with where to look ("In the SW, 32° up"). A fist at arm's length is
about 10°.

If the Sun is up, or it is still twilight, the screen says so: the stars are there but will
not be seen until the Sun is about 6° below the horizon.

## Finding north

The drawing is the sky towards the pole, for your place at that moment.

- **North of the equator:** find the Plough. The two stars at the end of its bowl, Merak and
  Dubhe, point to Polaris, about five times the distance between them. Polaris is within a
  degree of true north; drop a line straight down from it to the horizon. Cassiopeia, the W,
  is on the other side of Polaris and is higher when the Plough is low.
- **South of the equator:** there is no bright pole star. Carry the long axis of the Southern
  Cross, from Gacrux at its foot through Acrux at its head, on about four and a half times its
  length. That point is the south pole of the sky; below it on the horizon is true south.

The pole stands as high above the horizon as your latitude, so close to the equator it is on
the horizon and may be hidden by haze. Mark the direction with two sticks to keep it for the
day. This is true north, not the compass's; [Compass](tools-compass.md) gives the difference.

## How accurate it is

The stars' places come from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue, carried on by each star's own
motion and turned for precession, nutation and the aberration of light. The Sun, Moon and
planets come from their orbits, with the largest disturbances of the Moon, Jupiter and
Saturn. Held to the US Naval Observatory's almanac at five moments between 2026 and 2035:

| | Within |
| --- | --- |
| Stars | half a minute of arc |
| Sun | half a minute of arc |
| Moon, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn | two minutes of arc |
| Moonrise and moonset | three minutes of time |
| New, full and quarter moons | ten minutes of time |

A minute of arc is a thirtieth of the Moon's width, far finer than the chart can show.

## What it does not do

- Stars fainter than magnitude 4.5, Uranus, Neptune, comets, satellites and meteor showers.
- The air on the night: haze and refraction near the horizon move what is seen.
- Saturn's rings are left out of its brightness, which can make it up to a magnitude brighter
  than shown.
