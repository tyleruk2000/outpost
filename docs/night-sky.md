# Night sky

**Tools → Night sky** shows the Moon, a chart of the stars and planets over a place now or at
another time, and how to find north (or south) by the stars. It works with no internet.

Choose the place (home, a saved place or coordinates) and the clock, shared with the Solar
clock. **Now** follows the device's clock; **Another time** takes a date and time.

## The Moon

The phase, how much is lit, moonrise and moonset between midday and the next midday, and the
next new, first quarter, full and last quarter moons. Rise and set are for a level horizon:
hills and buildings make the Moon appear later and vanish sooner.

## The chart

The centre is overhead and the rim the horizon. North is at the top and **east on the
left**: hold it over your head with the top towards north. Stars down to magnitude 4.5 are
shown, which a dark country sky shows easily and a town sky does not; 27 constellations are
joined up. Planets are filled dots, the Moon an open circle.

**What is up** lists the Moon, planets and ten brightest stars above the horizon, with where
to look ("In the SW, 32° up"). A fist at arm's length is about 10°. While the Sun is up or
it is twilight, the screen says the stars will not be seen until the Sun is about 6° down.

## Finding north

- **North of the equator:** the two stars at the end of the Plough's bowl, Merak and Dubhe,
  point to Polaris, about five times the gap between them. Polaris is within a degree of true
  north. Cassiopeia, the W, is opposite the Plough.
- **South of the equator:** carry the long axis of the Southern Cross, from Gacrux through
  Acrux, about four and a half times its length. Below that point is true south.

The pole stands as high as your latitude, so near the equator it may be lost in haze. Mark
the direction with two sticks. This is true north; [Compass](tools-compass.md) gives the
difference from the compass.

## How accurate it is

Against the US Naval Observatory for 2026 to 2035: stars and the Sun within half a minute of
arc, the Moon and planets within two (a thirtieth of the Moon's width), moonrise and moonset
within three minutes of time, the Moon's phases within ten.

Not shown: stars fainter than 4.5, Uranus, Neptune, comets, satellites and meteors; haze and
refraction near the horizon. Saturn's rings are left out of its brightness, so it can be up
to a magnitude brighter than shown.
