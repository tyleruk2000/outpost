# Solar clock

**Tools → Solar clock** tells the time from the sun and the stars, worked out on the device
with no internet. **Never look at the sun directly.** These methods are for approximate
timekeeping, not for navigation or anything where safety depends on the time.

Above the tabs choose the place (home, a saved place, or typed coordinates) and the clock
the answers are written on: this device's time zone, or a fixed UTC offset if the device's
zone is wrong. Both are remembered on this device.

## Now

The sun's height and bearing, sundial time beside clock time, the equation of time,
today's solar noon and the sun's path. It trusts the device's clock; if that is in doubt,
use **Clock recovery**.

## Clock recovery

For after a power cut, with no internet or signal. Type the date, then measure something
with a stick, level ground, string and a ruler. The answer is the time to set the clocks
by, with a range and what the range comes from. Nothing on this tab reads the device's
clock. The methods, best first:

| Method | What you measure | How good |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noon, two equal shadows | The moments, morning and afternoon, when the shadow's tip touches one circle round the stick, on any running clock | About a minute with a 1 m stick, a 4 m circle and 5 mm marking. Gives how far the running clock is out. |
| Noon, the shortest shadow | The moment the shadow is shortest | Poor: near noon the shadow hardly changes (18 minutes either side in June at Greenwich). |
| The shadow's length | Stick and shadow, morning or afternoon | Good two hours or more from noon; within about 75 minutes of noon use a noon method. |
| The shadow's bearing | Compass bearing from the stick's foot to the tip | Depends on the compass. The magnetic declination is filled in from the World Magnetic Model; you can change it. |
| Sunrise or sunset on the sea | Which, and your eye's height above the sea | A rough check only. |

**If the date is not certain**, it shows how far each answer moves if the date is 1, 3 or 7
days out. Times are given to no more figures than their range deserves. **Better sources**
points to radio time signals (**Radio → Frequencies**), a GPS receiver, and a phone that
has had signal.

The default marking errors (5 mm with a 1 m stick, 2° on a compass) are Outpost's guess at
careful work.

## Make a sundial

A horizontal dial for your latitude: the hour lines in a table, finding true north from a
shadow, the correction table from sundial to clock time for the place, a card-and-pencil
equatorial dial, and a template to print (the dial plate, and the gnomon to cut out with
the set-up steps). Not offered within 1° of the equator, where every hour line is the noon
line; use the card dial there.

## Make a nocturnal

A nocturnal tells the time at night from the Plough turning about Polaris. The tab explains
it, prints a template made for the place (a date disc, an hour disc and arm), and **Read
the star clock now** works out the time from the angle of Dubhe from Polaris. The printed
nocturnal takes Polaris to be at the pole, which costs 6 to 12 minutes depending on
latitude. There is no star clock for the south: no checkable method for the Southern Cross
was found.

Templates fit A4 and US Letter, with a 100 mm bar to check the printer did not scale them.

## How accurate it is

The sun's height and bearing agree with the US Naval Observatory to about 0.01°, and
sunrise, sunset and solar noon to within a minute between 72° N and S (ten minutes
beyond). Inside the polar circles, the polar night and midnight sun are said in words. Star
places are for 2026 to 2046 and drift slowly outside those years.
